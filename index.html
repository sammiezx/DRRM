    I

    The Ground

    full act →

    Nepal is being built by the same process that destroys it, and risk is something we construct, not something that befalls us.

    Must know
    • India converges on Tibet at 18–20 mm/yr, locked on the Main Himalayan Thrust. Strain accumulates until released.
    • 1934, 2015 — and the far west has not had a great earthquake since 1505. 2015 released nothing there.
    • For a road engineer, an earthquake arrives as tens of thousands of landslides at once. Gorkha triggered around 25,000.
    • Risk = hazard × exposure × vulnerability ÷ capacity. You cannot change the hazard — except that badly built roads create it.
    • Four physiographic belts, four different failure modes. Know which one you are standing in.
    • Hazards arrive in chains: earthquake → landslide → river blockage → outburst flood. The destructive link is usually the last one.
    Traps
    • "We had ours in 2015." Wrong segment, and not the maximum event.
    • Comfortable, terraced Middle Hills ground is often dormant colluvium — an old slide waiting for a toe cut.
    • "Natural disaster" attributes the outcome to the weather, and the reconstruction then rebuilds the same vulnerability.

    You do: know your corridor's critical link, and argue for alignment and redundancy ahead of marginal strengthening — because the failure mode is a severed network, not a cracked member.

    II

    Reading the Terrain

    full act →

    A slope is a balance. Rain does not add weight — it removes strength, so the trigger is never the cause.

    Must know
    • τ = c′ + (σ − u) tan φ′. Friction comes from σ − u. Raise pore pressure and the slope weakens without getting heavier.
    • Shallow failures follow rainfall intensity — hours. Deep-seated follow accumulation — days to weeks. The clear morning after is dangerous.
    • Five movement types: fall, topple, slide, spread, flow. A slide has one surface and a meaningful factor of safety; a flow has neither.
    • Debris flow ≈ 1.8–2.3 t/m³, entrains its channel and grows. The quiet gully is the loaded gully.
    • Six field signs, read crown down: tension cracks, scarp, tilted trees, hummocky ground, changed water, bulging toe.
    • Rock fails on geometry, not strength — the same cut on the other side of the valley stands.
    Traps
    • A cut face standing through two dry seasons is held by matric suction, not strength. Saturation removes it entirely.
    • A wall founded above the slip surface is a passenger.
    • A susceptibility map is not a risk map, and a regional map cannot clear a site.

    You do: walk to the crown, not just the road. Seal the cracks you find, mark them with a date — and treat every slope problem as a water problem until the ground proves otherwise.

    The uncomfortable one: most landslides on Nepal's hill roads were manufactured by the road — cut, spoil, concentrated water, no maintenance. Failures cluster at drain outlets, and a natural hazard has no reason to prefer them.

    III

    Water

    full act →

    A Himalayan river is a sediment conveyor that happens to contain water. Design for the water alone and you will be right about the hydraulics and wrong about the river.

    Must know
    • The critical storm has duration = time of concentration. Hill kholas run 10–30 min. Using a 24-hour intensity underestimates badly.
    • A 50-year design on a 50-year life is exceeded 64 % of the time. Design for what happens then.
    • Use two methods — not to average, but to catch factor-of-two errors.
    • Bed load fills your waterway. Aggradation reduces it; degradation undermines it.
    • Scour peaks at the flood and refills as it recedes. Low-water inspection is built to mislead. Founding depth is the only defence needing no maintenance.
    • Bank protection fails from the toe up and the ends in. The launching apron is sacrificial by design.
    Traps
    • The Koshi left its course in 2008 below capacity — avulsion is a breach, not a capacity failure. Site for the belt, not the channel.
    • A debris raft can double local scour. On a Nepali river in monsoon that is the ordinary case.
    • You cannot economically build a road structure for a GLOF. Elevation and expendability are the only affordable defence.

    You do: get tc before sizing anything, survey the bed at the same sections every year after monsoon, and put the design discharge, return period, method and date on the drawing.

    IV

    The Road That Survives

    full act →

    Alignment is the design; everything after it is consequence management — and a road is a machine for collecting water.

    Must know
    • Alignment is the only lever that changes exposure, and it also governs how much new hazard the road creates. Free at corridor stage, impossible after construction.
    • Avoid: old landslides, debris fans, adverse dip, river migration corridors, seepage, long high cuts. Ridge lines are under-used.
    • Drainage: catch drain above the cut (usually absent, most valuable), lined side drain, frequent turnouts, and every outlet reaching a natural channel.
    • Cross-drainage: size for debris, not discharge. One big opening beats several small. Nothing you cannot enter to clean.
    • Walls fail from water behind and ground beneath — weep holes, free-draining backfill and a filter, all three or none.
    • Bio-engineering works only inside the root zone (0.5–1.5 m). Below that, nothing reaches.
    • Green Roads: mass balance and no side-tipping — two clauses that survive being handed to a contractor with an excavator.
    • Bridges are lost below the bearing line. Seat width and restrainers are nearly free at design and cannot be added later.
    Traps
    • A gabion wall is the default answer and is wrong perhaps a third of the time. Establish that a wall is the answer first.
    • Shortening a bridge moves cost underground — narrowing the waterway 25 % drove the founding level 3.5 m deeper.
    • A resilient design that depends on maintenance nobody funds is not resilient. Design for the maintenance you will actually get.

    You do: three questions on any hill road — where does the spoil go, where does every drain end, and who clears them before the monsoon. That finds more risk in an afternoon than a week of analysis.

    V

    Beyond the Alignment

    full act →

    Access is the response. And the engineering is necessary but not sufficient.

    Must know
    • Resilience is bought with topology, not concrete. Identify your critical link and its alternative — most districts never have.
    • First 72 hours: your product is information and triage, not repair. Ask "can a loaded truck get from X to Y, and if not, where does it stop?"
    • Quick wins reaching many people first. Bypass before clearance. One lane, not two.
    • Buildings, four killers visible from the street: soft storey, short column, torsional irregularity, pounding. Ask which edition of NBC 105.
    • Masonry: bands at plinth, sill, lintel, roof; corner reinforcement; through-stones; gable band.
    • Reconstruction is the moment risk can be reduced and the moment of maximum pressure to rebuild what was there. The window is narrow.
    • Land use is exposure; maintenance and funding are capacity. Both are cheaper than anything in Act IV.
    Traps
    • The resources that repair the network depend on the network. One loader behind the critical link is no capacity at all.
    • Emergency reinstatement becomes permanent by default unless you record in writing that it is temporary.
    • Blanket red zones get ignored. Conditions get followed.

    You do: hold one line without apology — nobody works under a slide that is still moving. Keep the notebook in the first days; the recovery programme will be built on whatever was written down.

    If you remember five things
    1. Water is the mechanism. In slopes, in walls, in roads, in bridges — it is almost always the water.
    2. Alignment beats everything. Free early, impossible late, and the only lever on exposure.
    3. Scour, not shaking, takes bridges. Found below total scour and stop economising there.
    4. The road often causes the landslide. Cut, spoil, concentrated water. Failures cluster at outlets.
    5. Design for the maintenance you will actually get, and for what happens on the day you are exceeded.
    Act I

    The Ground

    Why this matters, before any engineering.

    1 · The eighty-year clock

    Session 1 · 13 min

    On 15 January 1934, a great earthquake struck eastern Nepal and north Bihar. It levelled much of Bhaktapur, damaged Kathmandu heavily, and killed somewhere in the order of ten thousand people across the region. Eighty-one years later, on 25 April 2015, the Gorkha earthquake killed around nine thousand and damaged or destroyed hundreds of thousands of buildings. Between those two dates a country was rebuilt, roaded and largely re-forgotten. The reason this book opens here is not history. It is that the segment of the fault that matters most to western Nepal has not moved in far longer than either of those intervals — and everything you build sits on top of that fact.

    The idea

    Nepal is being built by the same process that destroys it. The Himalaya rises because India is being driven under Tibet, and the strain that lifts the range is released as earthquakes. For a road engineer the earthquake does not arrive as shaking — it arrives as tens of thousands of landslides at once.

    The machine underneath

    India is converging on Tibet at roughly 18 to 20 mm per year. About half of that is taken up across the Himalaya itself, on a single great surface — the Main Himalayan Thrust — which dips gently north beneath the whole country. Everything you know as Nepal's geology is the wreckage above it, stacked into slices by faults that branch off it and reach the surface: the MFT at the front of the Siwaliks, the MBT, the MCT further north.

    The thrust is locked over a broad band beneath the Lesser Himalaya. Locked means it is not slipping, while India keeps pushing — so strain accumulates, year after year, until it is released. At roughly 20 mm a year of convergence, a century of quiet stores something in the order of a metre of slip waiting to happen. That is the clock in this session's title, and it does not reset because a different segment moved.

    Read the middle of that table carefully. Far-western Nepal has no great earthquake in its historical record since 1505. Five centuries of convergence at 20 mm a year is a very large amount of stored slip. This is the central seismic gap, and it is the single most important piece of context for anyone building infrastructure in the west of the country.

    Why 2015 did not fix anything

    Gorkha ruptured a patch of the thrust east and north of the epicentre, propagating towards Kathmandu. It was a serious event and it did enormous damage. But two things about it are routinely misread:

    • It did not rupture to the surface along the front of the range, and it did not release the locked segment to the west. The strain there is untouched.
    • It was not the maximum credible event. A 7.8 is not the largest earthquake this system produces. 1934 was larger, and the palaeoseismic record contains larger still.

    So "we have had ours" is not available as a comfort, and it is worth being blunt when you hear it — because you will, from clients and from committees.

    What an earthquake actually does to a road

    Here is the translation that matters for your work. Shaking damages buildings. On steep ground, shaking destabilises slopes — and it does so across an entire region simultaneously.

    Gorkha triggered on the order of twenty-five thousand mapped landslides. Roads were not cut in one or two places; they were cut in hundreds, which meant the machinery needed to clear them could not reach most of them. The Araniko Highway, the Pasang Lhamu route to the north, and the roads through Sindhupalchok, Rasuwa and Dhading were severed repeatedly. Access — which is the whole of disaster response — collapsed at exactly the moment it was needed.

    Three consequences follow, and each one is a session later in this book:

    • Co-seismic landslides do not stop when the shaking does. Slopes loosened by an earthquake keep failing through the following monsoons, for years. The hazard after Gorkha ran well beyond 2015.
    • Landslide dams form. Slides block rivers, lakes build behind them, and the dams later breach — session 16's LDOF, and the reason Jure in session 6 was a national emergency rather than a local one.
    • Redundancy decides the outcome. Whether a district can be reached at all after an event is a road-network question, not a bridge-design question. Session 28.

    The shaking is not the design problem for most of what you build. A well-founded culvert does not much care about ground motion. What removes it is the hillside above it arriving, or the river below it changing. For a road engineer in Nepal, the seismic hazard is mostly a slope hazard and a network hazard — which is why this book spends one session on buildings and seven on terrain.

    Judgment

    Carry two facts into every conversation about risk in this country. First, the strain is still there, and the west has been quiet the longest — so "recently affected" and "currently at risk" are close to opposites. Second, when the event comes, your problem will be a thousand slope failures and a severed network, not a cracked abutment. That should change what you argue for: alignment away from unstable ground, redundant links, and structures that can be reopened quickly, ahead of marginal strengthening of individual members.

    Field assignment

    Take one road corridor you know well — twenty to fifty kilometres — and ask the network question rather than the structural one:

    1. If this corridor were cut in five places at once, which settlements would be unreachable, and for how long?
    2. Is there any alternative route to them, however poor? Mark it.
    3. Which single structure or slope, if lost, isolates the most people? That is your critical link.

    What you probably found: one link doing far more work than the others, and no alternative to it. Almost every corridor has one. Knowing which one it is — and being able to name it in a meeting — is worth more than any amount of general concern about earthquakes.

    Check yourself

    1 · What is the Main Himalayan Thrust, and what does it mean that it is "locked"?

    It is the single gently north-dipping surface on which India underthrusts Tibet, with the MFT, MBT and MCT branching off it to the surface. Locked means the shallow part is not slipping while convergence of about 18–20 mm/yr continues — so strain accumulates until it is released as an earthquake.

    2 · Someone says Nepal "had its earthquake in 2015". Give the two-part answer.

    Gorkha ruptured one segment and left the locked zone to the west untouched, so that strain is still stored — far-western Nepal has no great event in the record since 1505. And 7.8 is not the maximum this system produces; 1934 was larger. Neither the location nor the size makes 2015 a release of the outstanding risk.

    3 · For a road engineer, how does an earthquake actually arrive?

    As tens of thousands of simultaneous landslides. Gorkha triggered on the order of 25,000. The network is cut in hundreds of places at once, so clearing machinery cannot reach most of them — access fails exactly when it is needed.

    4 · Why does the landslide hazard not end when the shaking stops?

    Because shaking loosens slopes that then fail in subsequent monsoons, for years afterwards. It also creates landslide dams, which impound lakes that later breach as outburst floods — a hazard that arrives long after the earthquake.

    5 · What does this session imply you should argue for, ahead of strengthening individual members?

    Alignment away from unstable ground, redundancy in the network, and structures that can be reopened quickly. If the failure mode is a severed network rather than a cracked element, network decisions buy more resilience per rupee than marginal structural capacity.

    2 · Risk is built, not suffered

    Session 2 · 13 min

    Two villages, one valley, the same storm. In the first, a slope fails above the settlement and eleven people die. In the second — same rock, same rainfall, same slope angle — a slope fails above the settlement and nobody does. Reports will call both a natural disaster. Only one of those words is doing any work. The hazard was identical; the outcome was not, and everything that differed between the two was built, decided or neglected by people. That is not a moral point. It is the reason an engineer has a job here at all.

    The idea

    Risk is the product of a hazard, what is exposed to it, and how vulnerable that exposure is — divided by the capacity to cope. The hazard is the one term you cannot touch. Everything you can change sits in the other three, and that is the entire scope of the profession.

    The four terms

    The relation everybody quotes is not an equation you compute — it is a checklist for finding where the leverage is:

    Risk = (Hazard × Exposure × Vulnerability) ÷ Capacity
    TermMeansCan an engineer change it?
    HazardThe damaging phenomenon — its magnitude and how often it occursNo. You cannot reduce the rainfall or unload the fault. You can, occasionally, reduce a local hazard you created — see below
    ExposureWhat is in the way: people, houses, the road, the bridge, the cropsYes, decisively. Alignment, siting, land use. The cheapest reduction available
    VulnerabilityHow badly the exposed thing is damaged when the hazard reaches itYes. This is design, detailing, construction quality, maintenance
    CapacityThe ability to anticipate, absorb and recover — warning, funds, skills, redundancy, organisationPartly. Redundant routes, maintainable structures, early warning, training

    Two consequences fall straight out of the table, and they are worth stating as rules:

    If exposure is zero, risk is zero — whatever the hazard. A catastrophic landslide on an empty hillside is a geomorphological event, not a disaster. This is why session 12 insists that a susceptibility map is not a risk map, and why session 20 argues that alignment is the largest single risk decision on any road.

    The hazard term is the only one nobody chose. Which means that when a disaster happens, the interesting question is never "how big was the event" but "which of the other three did we get wrong, and when did we decide it".

    The exception: hazards you created

    Engineers are usually taught they cannot influence the hazard. In hill road work that is not quite true, and the exception is the most important idea in this book.

    A slope that would never have failed can be made to fail by a cut, by tipped spoil, by a drain discharging onto it. That is not increasing exposure or vulnerability — it is manufacturing a new hazard where there was none. Session 10 is entirely about this. It means the profession's relationship to the hazard term is not neutral: we can add to it, and frequently do.

    Which gives you a genuinely useful question to ask of any project: does this work reduce risk, or does it move risk around, or does it create new hazard? A road that brings a clinic within reach reduces enormous risk. The same road, badly built, creates landslide hazard for everyone downhill of it. Both are true at once, and honest engineering means holding both.

    Vulnerability is a social fact, not only a physical one

    It is tempting to treat vulnerability as purely structural — how strong is the wall, how deep the foundation. In practice it is also about who lives where, and why.

    In the Nepali hills, the households on the least stable ground are very often the ones with the fewest options: the land was cheap because it was steep, or it was what was left, or it is the only plot within reach of work. The same event kills disproportionately among people with mud-mortar stone housing, no savings and no second option. That is why the Jajarkot earthquake in 2023, at a magnitude that would have done modest damage elsewhere, was as lethal as it was.

    You do not need to become a sociologist. You do need to recognise two operational consequences:

    • Relocation is rarely the simple answer it looks like. "Move the settlement" assumes there is somewhere to move to, that it is owned, and that livelihoods survive the move. Session 32 returns to this.
    • The cheapest intervention often wins on risk reduction per rupee even if it is unsatisfying technically — a drain re-cut before monsoon, a crown crack sealed, a warning arrangement that works. Perfect is frequently the enemy of many small reductions.

    Where the money should go

    The four terms rank interventions for you, and the ranking is stable across almost every project:

    1. Reduce exposure — do not put it there. Alignment, siting, land use. Free at design stage, impossible later.
    2. Avoid creating hazard — build the cut, spoil and drainage properly. Cheap at construction, ruinous to retrofit.
    3. Reduce vulnerability — design, detail, and above all maintain. Ongoing cost, reliable return.
    4. Build capacity — redundancy, warning, funded maintenance, trained people. Slow, unglamorous, and the thing that determines whether the other three survive contact with reality.

    Notice that the two highest-value items are both decisions taken early and cost almost nothing at the time they are taken. That asymmetry — cheap now, unaffordable later — is the strongest argument you will ever have in a project meeting, and it is worth learning to make it in one sentence.

    "Natural disaster" is a phrase worth retiring. The hazard is natural. The disaster is what happens when a natural hazard meets choices about where people live, what they live in, and what was maintained. Using the phrase in a report quietly attributes the outcome to the weather, and the reconstruction that follows will then rebuild the same vulnerability. This is not pedantry — it is the difference between a report that changes something and one that does not.

    Judgment

    When you are handed a problem, run the four terms before you reach for a structure. Ask which term the proposed intervention actually attacks, and whether a cheaper one attacks a bigger term. A great deal of money in this country is spent reducing vulnerability — walls, protection works — at sites where the exposure should never have been there, and where the hazard was created by an earlier construction decision. Getting the diagnosis right is most of the value you add; the detailing comes after.

    Field assignment

    Take one place you know that has been damaged — a road section, a settlement, a crossing. Write four short lines, one per term:

    1. Hazard — what happened, and was any of it manufactured?
    2. Exposure — what was in the way, and who put it there?
    3. Vulnerability — why did it damage so easily?
    4. Capacity — what made recovery slow or fast?

    Then answer one question: which single term, changed twenty years ago, would have made the biggest difference?

    What you probably found: exposure — a decision about where something was put, taken long before anyone thought about the hazard. That is the usual answer, and it is why the alignment session sits at the head of Act IV rather than buried in it.

    Check yourself

    1 · Name the four terms and say which one an engineer generally cannot change.

    Hazard, exposure, vulnerability and capacity. Hazard is generally beyond influence — you cannot reduce the rainfall or unload the fault. The other three are all products of human decisions, and they are the entire scope of the profession.

    2 · What is the exception to "an engineer cannot change the hazard", and why does it matter so much in hill roads?

    Construction can manufacture hazard that did not exist — a cut that removes toe support, spoil tipped onto a stable slope, a drain discharging onto open hillside. It matters because it means our relationship to the hazard term is not neutral: we can add to it, and on badly built hill roads we routinely do.

    3 · Why is a catastrophic landslide on an empty hillside not a disaster?

    Because exposure is zero, so risk is zero regardless of the hazard's size. It is a geomorphological event. This is also why a susceptibility map is not a risk map — it describes terrain, not consequence.

    4 · Rank the four kinds of intervention by value, and say what the top two have in common.

    Reduce exposure, avoid creating hazard, reduce vulnerability, build capacity. The top two are both decisions taken early that cost almost nothing at the time and become unaffordable or impossible later — which is the strongest argument available in a project meeting.

    5 · What is wrong with calling an event a "natural disaster" in a report?

    The hazard is natural; the disaster is what happens when it meets decisions about where people live, what they live in, and what was maintained. The phrase attributes the outcome to the weather, and reconstruction then rebuilds the same vulnerability.

    3 · Why Nepal's hills move

    Session 3 · 14 min

    Drive north from the Terai and you cross four different countries in a hundred and fifty kilometres. Flat alluvium, then a belt of soft young sandstone and conglomerate that erodes if you look at it, then deeply weathered schist and phyllite cut into terraces, then hard rock and ice. Engineers who work one belt often carry its habits into another — and the failure that follows looks like bad luck. It is not. Each belt fails in its own way, wants a different cut angle, a different wall and a different drainage strategy, and knowing which one you are standing in is the beginning of competence here.

    The idea

    Nepal's instability comes from four things acting together: rock that is young and broken, ground that is steep, four months of concentrated rain, and a range that is still rising. Nothing is wrong with the hills. They are behaving exactly as that combination requires — and the physiographic belt you are in tells you how.

    The four ingredients

    Young, deformed, broken rock. The Himalaya has been assembled by thrusting, which means the rock has been sheared, folded and fractured on every scale. What governs a slope is rarely intact rock strength — it is the discontinuities, which is session 11. Add deep chemical weathering in a warm wet climate and much of the Middle Hills is mantled in weathered material and old colluvium: loose, previously moved, and often sitting at close to its natural angle already.

    Steep ground. Relief of several thousand metres over short horizontal distances. Gravity has more to work with here than almost anywhere.

    Concentrated rain. Roughly 80 % of annual rainfall arrives in the four monsoon months, June to September. The distribution across the country is extreme — parts of the southern slopes around Pokhara receive of the order of 4,000–5,000 mm a year, while Manang and Mustang, in the rain shadow behind the main range, receive a few hundred. Two design consequences: annual averages are almost useless to you, and the same structure detail is being asked to do wildly different jobs in different districts.

    Active uplift. The range is rising, so rivers are steepening and cutting down. A river incising its bed removes support from the toe of every slope along it — which is session 6's mechanism, operating continuously, for free, without any help from us. Hillsides in Nepal are not in long-term equilibrium. They are in a slow permanent adjustment to a base level that keeps dropping.

    The two belts that catch people out

    The Siwaliks. Engineers trained on Middle Hills work often underestimate them badly. The rock is soft enough to cut easily — which is exactly the trap, because a face that stands beautifully in the dry season ravels away in the first monsoon and keeps going. Vegetation establishes poorly on it. The sediment yield is so high that structures downstream in the Terai are being sized against material this belt is delivering. If your alignment crosses the Churia, expect to spend on erosion control what you would spend on stability further north.

    The Middle Hills. The trap here is the opposite: the ground looks settled. Terraces, villages, mature trees, centuries of cultivation. But a very large proportion of that gentle-looking ground is old landslide debris — colluvium that moved once and has been quiet since. It is dormant, not stable, in the exact sense of session 7. Cut a road through its toe and you have reactivated a mass that has been waiting.

    What this means for standards

    Because the belts differ so much, a single national cut slope table or a single drainage detail cannot be right everywhere. It is right somewhere and approximately wrong elsewhere. This is not a criticism of the standards — a standard has to generalise — but it does mean two things for you:

    • Know which belt the standard's default was calibrated for, and be ready to argue for a departure when you are outside it.
    • Trust the local evidence over the table. The existing cuts, walls and failures within a few kilometres of your site were an experiment somebody already ran in your material. Session 9's eye, applied to other people's work.

    Deforestation, terracing and abandonment all change the ground. The hills you are working in are not a natural system — they have been terraced, irrigated, grazed and, more recently, abandoned as people migrate. Abandoned terraces are a real and growing hazard: their retaining risers and drainage stop being maintained, water ponds and concentrates where it used to be led away, and slopes that were stable under management start to fail. When a local tells you a slope "never used to do this", ask what changed in the land use, not only in the rainfall.

    Judgment

    Start every project by naming the belt and the material, out loud, before any design parameter is chosen — and if the alignment crosses a boundary, treat the two parts as two projects. Then look at what already exists nearby: the cuts that stood, the walls that failed, the angle that vegetation holds on. In ground this variable, a survey of local precedent is worth more than a table, and it takes an afternoon. And be especially careful of gentle, cultivated, comfortable-looking Middle Hills ground, because a good deal of it is an old slide that has not been asked a question in a hundred years.

    Field assignment

    Take a corridor you know that crosses at least one belt boundary. Then:

    1. Mark roughly where the boundary falls, using the ground rather than a map — the change in rock, in slope form, in cultivation.
    2. On each side, note the dominant failure type you can actually see: ravelling and gullying, slides, rockfall, or none.
    3. Note whether the cut slope angles and wall types change at the boundary. Did the designer notice it?

    What you probably found: the ground changed and the standard detail did not. That is extremely common, and it is one of the more defensible things to raise on a design review, because the evidence is visible from the road.

    Check yourself

    1 · Name the four ingredients that make Nepal's hills unstable.

    Young, deformed and deeply weathered rock; very steep ground; rainfall concentrated into four monsoon months; and active uplift, which steepens rivers so they incise and continually remove toe support from the slopes above them.

    2 · Why are annual average rainfall figures of limited use in Nepal?

    Because about 80% of it falls in four months, and the spatial variation is extreme — several thousand millimetres on the southern slopes against a few hundred in the rain-shadow districts. An annual mean conceals both the concentration and the geography that actually drive design.

    3 · What is the trap in the Siwaliks?

    The rock is soft and cuts easily, so faces stand well in the dry season — then ravel away through the monsoon and keep going. Vegetation establishes poorly, and the sediment yield is enormous. It demands erosion control rather than the stability engineering that suits ground further north.

    4 · Why is comfortable-looking Middle Hills ground dangerous?

    Much of it is old colluvium — landslide debris that moved once and has been quiet since. It is dormant, not stable. Terraces, villages and mature trees make it look settled, and cutting a road through its toe is a reliable way to reactivate it.

    5 · Why can a single national cut-slope or drainage detail not be right everywhere in Nepal?

    Because the four belts differ so fundamentally in material and failure mode that a generalised default is calibrated for one and approximately wrong in the others. You need to know which belt it suits, and to weigh local precedent — the cuts and walls that already stood or failed nearby — over the table.

    4 · The rules of the game

    Session 4 · 13 min

    A ward chair wants a track opened to a settlement before the next election. There is money, there is a contractor with a dozer, and there is nobody in the room whose job it is to say no. Six years later the track has been re-cut four times, two hillsides below it are failing, and the district is asking why. Every part of that story is legal. The question worth understanding is not what the law forbids — it is who is responsible, and what they are required to do before they act, because that is the only lever you have when you are the one in the room.

    The idea

    Since federalisation, most road-building authority in Nepal sits with local government, and most disaster risk management authority sits with a structure that runs in parallel to it. Knowing which body owns which decision — and which standard binds it — is what turns an engineer's objection into something that has to be answered.

    The disaster law

    The governing statute is the Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Act, 2074 (2017), with its Rules of 2076. It replaced a much older, response-focused arrangement, and its central move is in the name: from managing disasters to reducing risk. Three things it set up matter to you:

    • NDRRMA — the National Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Authority, the executive body that coordinates across ministries and levels.
    • A committee at every tier. A national council and executive committee, then province, then District Disaster Management Committees and Local Disaster Management Committees at municipality and rural municipality level. The local committee is the one you will actually deal with.
    • A duty that runs downwards. Local governments are not merely permitted to do risk reduction; they carry responsibility for it, including preparedness planning and response in their jurisdiction.

    Alongside the Act sits the Disaster Risk Reduction National Strategic Action Plan (2018–2030), which is Nepal's translation of the Sendai Framework into priorities and actions. Sendai matters less for its content than for what it does to funding: donors and national programmes report against its four priorities and seven targets, so an intervention framed in that language finds money more easily. That is a practical fact, not a cynical one, and it is worth knowing when you write a proposal.

    Who owns the road

    This is where engineers most often get lost, and it decides which standard applies.

    NetworkOwnerRoughly
    Strategic Road Network — highways and feeder roadsDepartment of Roads (federal)Designed, procured and supervised by DoR divisions. Bridge design to DoR standards and adopted IRC practice
    Provincial roadsProvince ministries and their infrastructure directoratesA newer tier, still consolidating capacity

    The consequential fact is the last row. That is the mechanism behind session 10's landscape of dozer-cut tracks. It is also why arguing standards at the federal level achieves little for the network doing most of the damage: the decision you need to influence is being taken in a rural municipality office.

    The standards that bind

    Know which document governs before you argue about a number:

    • Nepal Road Standard — geometric and general standards for the strategic network, issued by DoR.
    • Nepal Rural Road Standard — the counterpart for local roads, issued for the local network. This is the document that matters for most hill track construction, and the one least often opened.
    • DoR bridge standards and directives, with IRC codes as adopted, for bridge design, hydrology and foundations.
    • Environment-friendly / Green Roads guidance for labour-based hill road construction — session 25.
    • Environmental law — the Environment Protection Act and its rules require screening, and IEE or EIA depending on the scale and sensitivity of a project. For a hill road this is the formal hook on which spoil disposal, slope stability and drainage obligations actually hang.
    • The National Building Code — session 30, and enforced by municipalities through the building permit process under the Building Act.

    Editions change. Every one of these is revised. Cite the edition and the date in your submissions, and check the current version before you rely on a value. An argument won with a superseded clause is a loss with a delay on it.

    Where the leverage actually is

    Given all that, four levers work in practice, roughly in order of how often they succeed:

    1. The environmental process. IEE and EIA requirements are the most reliable formal point at which spoil disposal, drainage and slope stability must be addressed on paper. Engineers under-use this because it looks like paperwork. It is the paperwork with teeth.
    2. The local disaster committee. A DDMC or LDMC has a legal remit for risk reduction in its jurisdiction. An engineer's written assessment that a proposed alignment creates hazard is something that committee has to receive — and once it is minuted, the decision has an owner.
    3. The standard as a floor. Rural road standards are frequently unmet rather than unknown. "This does not meet NRRS" is a stronger sentence than "this is unwise", because the first has a document behind it.
    4. Maintenance funding. The most useful thing you can attach to any hill road approval is a funded, named maintenance responsibility. Without it, session 10 plays out regardless of how well the road was designed.
    Judgment

    Write your objection down, address it to the body that owns the decision, and say which standard or requirement is not being met. Verbal concern from an engineer is absorbed without trace; a written note to a municipality or a DDMC citing NRRS, an IEE obligation or a DRRM Act responsibility creates a record and transfers the risk to whoever overrules it. That is not bureaucratic self-protection — it is the mechanism by which anything changes. And when you are the one being asked to approve, remember that most of the damage in this country is done by works that were entirely legal.

    Field assignment

    Pick one road near you that was built in the last ten years and establish, by asking rather than guessing:

    1. Who owns it — DoR, province, or the municipality?
    2. Was there a design, and was there environmental screening?
    3. Who is responsible for maintaining it, and is there a budget line?

    What you probably found: local ownership, no meaningful design, and no funded maintenance. That combination is the single best predictor of the failures in session 10 — and because each of the three has a named owner, each is something an engineer can actually raise with somebody.

    5 · The cascade

    Session 5 · 14 min

    On 2 August 2014 a hillside failed at Jure. That was a landslide, and it killed around 156 people. But the landslide was not the end of the event. The debris dammed the Sunkoshi, a lake began to fill behind it, and for weeks the real question was not the slope — it was whether a dam nobody designed was about to release into a valley with a highway, settlements and a hydropower plant in it. Downstream districts were warned. The army blasted a channel through it. One hazard had become a second, larger one, sixty kilometres from where it started.

    The idea

    In mountain terrain hazards arrive in chains, not singly. Each link can be larger and reach further than the one before it — so the hazard that damages your work is often not the hazard that started, and it may originate far outside anything you assessed.

    The chains that matter in Nepal

    Earthquake → landslides → landslide dams → outburst floods. The Gorkha sequence in session 1, and Jure in miniature. Note the timescales are different at each link: seconds for the shaking, minutes to months for the slides, days to weeks for a dam to fill, minutes for it to release.

    Rainfall → slide → debris flow → channel blockage → flood. The everyday version, which happens somewhere in Nepal every monsoon. A slide enters a khola, mobilises into a flow, and either arrives at your crossing as session 8's boulder front or blocks the channel upstream and releases later.

    Glacier retreat → moraine lake → trigger → GLOF. Session 16. The chain that has no weather attached, and the one whose first link is now moving in one direction.

    Road construction → destabilised slope → slide → blocked river. Worth naming separately, because it is the one we start. Session 10's mechanism can initiate a chain that ends far downstream of the alignment that caused it.

    Why cascades break normal risk assessment

    Four reasons, and each one is a habit you have to unlearn:

    1. The magnitudes do not add — they multiply. A modest slide can impound a large volume of water. The energy released is not the energy of the slide; it is the energy of everything the slide organised behind it.
    2. The frequency is not in your record. Frequency analysis on a river gauge captures rainfall-driven floods. It cannot contain an outburst flood, because the mechanism is not the one that generated the record. Your 100-year discharge is a 100-year rainfall discharge, and it says nothing about a dam breach.
    3. The source is outside the study area. A crossing is assessed on its catchment. Cascades routinely begin upstream of anywhere the assessment looked, and arrive by a route nobody traced.
    4. The lag can be years. A slope loosened by an earthquake fails in the third monsoon afterwards. A structure that survived the event is taken out by its consequence, and the connection is not made in any report.

    What you actually do about it

    The temptation is despair — you cannot design for everything upstream. That is true, and three specific things remain worth doing:

    • Trace the chain upstream, once, on a map. For every significant crossing, ask what is above it: steep unstable ground, glacial lakes, a valley narrow enough to be dammed, a fan you are sitting on. Half a day. It will not change most designs and it will change a few completely.
    • Set levels from evidence, not only from hydrology. Session 16's rule. Deposits and trim lines record cascades that no gauge captured.
    • Design the network, not the structure. The reliable defence against a hazard you cannot predict is redundancy — an alternative route, a diversion, a structure that fails cheaply and reopens fast. Session 28.

    A landslide dam is an emergency the moment it forms. If a slide blocks a river anywhere upstream of your work, the operational questions are immediate: how big is the impoundment, how fast is it filling, who is downstream, and is anyone watching it. Landslide dams often fail within days or weeks. This is one of the few situations in this book where the correct engineering response is measured in hours, and where an engineer's early phone call to a district committee genuinely changes the outcome.

    Judgment

    Add one question to your standard assessment: what is upstream of this, and what could arrive here that did not start here? It costs half a day per crossing and it is the only way cascades enter your thinking at all, because no standard, no gauge record and no catchment analysis will put them there. And when you meet a fresh landslide dam, treat it as an active emergency with a downstream population, not as a geological curiosity to be assessed next week.

    Field assignment

    Take one crossing you are responsible for or know well, and trace its chain upstream on imagery:

    1. How far up does the catchment go, and what is the steepest, least stable ground in it?
    2. Is there anywhere the valley is narrow enough to be dammed by a slide from either side?
    3. Are there lakes, of any kind, above you?
    4. If the worst of those released, roughly how long would it take to reach you — and is there anyone who would know before you did?

    What you probably found: at least one narrow reach with unstable ground above it, and no arrangement whatsoever for anyone downstream to be told. The second finding is usually more actionable than the first, and it costs nothing to fix.

    Check yourself

    1 · Why was Jure a national emergency rather than a local landslide?

    Because the slide dammed the Sunkoshi and impounded a lake. The hazard became an outburst flood threatening a valley with a highway, settlements and a hydropower plant far downstream. The second link was larger and reached further than the first.

    2 · Why can frequency analysis on a river gauge not contain a GLOF or a dam-break flood?

    Because the record was generated by rainfall-driven floods, and an outburst flood is a different mechanism entirely. A 100-year discharge derived from that record is a 100-year rainfall discharge; it says nothing about a moraine or landslide dam releasing.

    3 · Give two reasons a cascade escapes a normal site assessment.

    Its source is usually outside the study area — assessments look at the catchment, while cascades begin upstream of anywhere examined. And the lag can be years: a slope loosened by an earthquake may fail in a later monsoon, so the connection is never made in any report.

    4 · A slide has just blocked a river upstream of your works. What are the immediate questions?

    How large is the impoundment, how fast is it filling, who is downstream, and is anyone monitoring it. Landslide dams often fail within days or weeks, so this is an active emergency requiring a call to the district committee — not an assessment to be scheduled.

    5 · What is the most reliable defence against a hazard you cannot predict?

    Redundancy — an alternative route, a diversion, a structure that fails cheaply and reopens fast. If you cannot forecast the event, you design the network so that losing any single element does not isolate anyone.

    6 · How a slope fails

    Session 6 · core · 15 min

    At about half past two in the morning on 2 August 2014, the hillside above Jure, in Sindhupalchok, let go. It took the village, roughly 156 people, and a length of the Araniko Highway. Then it did something worse: it dammed the Sunkoshi, and for weeks afterwards the country waited to see whether that dam would breach. It had rained hard that monsoon. But it had rained hard the week before too, and the hillside had stood. The question every engineer should ask about Jure is not why did it fail — it is why did it fail at 2:30 that morning, and not before?

    The idea

    A slope is a balance between the weight pulling it down and the strength holding it on. Rain does not add much weight. What it does is take strength away — and that is why the trigger and the cause are never the same thing.

    The balance

    Take any slope and imagine the surface it would slide on. Gravity resolves the mass above that surface into a component pushing along it. Against that, the ground resists with its shear strength. Divide one by the other and you have the only number the whole subject turns on:

    Factor of safety = resisting force ÷ driving force

    At FoS = 1.0 the slope is moving. Not "about to move" — moving. Everything above that is your margin against the things you got wrong.

    The resisting side is where the interesting physics lives. Shear strength on that surface follows the Mohr–Coulomb relation, and the form that matters is the effective stress form:

    τ = c′ + (σ − u) tan φ′

    Read that as three separate things you might be able to change:

    • c′ — effective cohesion. In most Nepali hill soils, small. In weathered rock and colluvium, often close to zero once it has moved once before.
    • σ — the total normal stress pressing the surface together. This is essentially the weight of what sits above.
    • u — the pore water pressure pushing the grains apart. This is the term that changes with the weather.

    Frictional strength does not come from σ. It comes from σ − u, the effective stress — the part of the load actually carried grain to grain. Water in the pores carries some of the load, and water has no friction. Raise u and you have not made the slope heavier in any meaningful way; you have made it weaker.

    Why the failure comes after the storm

    This is the part that catches people out, and it is worth being precise about, because it decides when you close a road.

    Water has to get from the surface down to the slip surface, and that takes time. How much time depends on how deep the surface is and how permeable the ground is. So slopes sort themselves into two families:

    FailureResponds toLag
    Shallow — debris slides, the 1–2 m skin of soil on a hillside or a cut faceRainfall intensity — the hard burstHours, sometimes minutes
    Deep-seated — the whole hillside, colluvium, old slide massesRainfall accumulation — how wet the ground already wasDays to weeks

    Which is why the dangerous morning is often the bright one. The storm passes, the sky clears, the road looks fine, the traffic goes back on — and the water is still working its way down. In the Middle Hills the big deep-seated movements cluster in late July and August, after the ground has had six weeks to saturate, not in the first storm of June.

    There is a second mechanism in the shallow family worth knowing by name. Unsaturated soil holds itself together partly by matric suction — the surface tension of water in partly filled pores, which acts like a temporary cohesion. It is why a cut face in silty soil can stand vertical for a season and look sound. Saturate it and that apparent cohesion vanishes completely. The face did not weaken gradually; it was never that strong, and it was being held up by something the first heavy monsoon removes.

    What you can actually change

    Go back to the balance and ask, honestly, which terms are available to you.

    • Gravity — no.
    • c′ and φ′ — essentially no. You cannot make the ground into different ground. (Soil nailing and grouting change the system, not the soil.)
    • Geometry — yes, and powerfully. Flatten the cut, bench it, unload the head, load the toe. Both sides of the ratio move at once, which is why it works.
    • u — yes, and cheaply. Every metre you drop the water table is strength you get back for the price of a drain.

    That last line is the whole reason drainage gets a session of its own, and the reason a gabion wall with no weep holes and no backfill drainage is not a retaining structure but a dam. You will meet that again in session 23.

    An honest word about the number. A factor of safety is a ratio between two quantities you estimated. It carries c′ and φ′ from a handful of samples, a slip surface you assumed, and a water table you guessed. FoS = 1.4 does not mean 40 % margin; it means 40 % margin if all of that was right. Design practice for permanent slopes generally looks for about 1.5 where the data is good, and accepts lower on temporary works — but a 1.5 built on a guessed water table is worth less than a 1.3 where you actually know where the water is. Confidence in the inputs matters more than the decimal.

    Judgment

    Treat every slope problem as a water problem until the ground proves otherwise. Before you price a wall, spend the morning finding out where water enters the slope, where it travels, and where it is supposed to leave. Half the failed structures on any hill road in Nepal are structurally sound walls that were asked to hold back water nobody drained. And when you are deciding whether to reopen a road: the rain stopping is not the all-clear. For a deep-seated mass it is the middle of the event, not the end.

    Field assignment

    Pick a slope failure you drive past — a scar on a cut, a slumped shoulder, anything. Stand where you can see the whole thing and answer three questions on paper:

    1. Where does water get in? Look uphill of the crown, not at the scar. A road above? A field being irrigated? A crown crack that funnels rain straight in?
    2. Where does it come out? Find seepage, damp patches that survive dry weather, or a line of greener vegetation across the face.
    3. Is the failure surface shallow or deep? Skin of soil, or has the whole mass moved?

    What you probably found: water entering from something man-made above the crown — a road drain, a diverted khola, a leaking irrigation channel — far more often than from rain falling on the scar itself. That is the ordinary case, not the exception, and it is the reason session 10 exists.

    Check yourself

    1 · Rain raises the pore pressure on a slip surface. Which term in the shear strength equation does that actually attack, and why does it matter that it is that one?

    The frictional term, tan φ′ — because friction is mobilised by the effective normal stress (σ − u), not the total stress σ. Cohesion c′ is untouched. So the wetter it gets, the more a slope that was relying on friction loses, while one relying on genuine cohesion loses less.

    2 · A cut face in silty soil has stood vertical for two dry seasons. Your junior says that proves it is stable. What is wrong with that?

    It was standing on matric suction — apparent cohesion from partly saturated pores — which disappears entirely on saturation. It was never strong; it was temporarily held. The first heavy monsoon is the real test.

    3 · Why do deep-seated failures often occur days after the heaviest rainfall, while debris slides occur during it?

    Because water needs time to reach the slip surface. A shallow surface responds to rainfall intensity within hours; a deep one responds to accumulated, antecedent rainfall over days or weeks. The lag is the travel time of the water, not a delay in the mechanics.

    4 · Of the terms in the stability balance, which two can a road engineer realistically change — and which of those two is cheaper?

    Geometry (cut angle, benching, unloading the head, loading the toe) and pore pressure (drainage). Drainage is far cheaper per unit of factor of safety recovered — which is why it is the first thing to design and the first thing to inspect.

    5 · You are asked whether to reopen a highway the morning after a three-day storm. The sky is clear and the road surface is intact. What do you say?

    That the surface tells you nothing about a deep-seated mass, which may still be gaining pore pressure. Look for crown cracks above the road, fresh seepage, tilted poles or trees, and offsets in the drain line. Clear weather is not the all-clear — for the deep failures it is often the middle of the event.

    7 · The five ways ground moves

    Session 7 · core · 14 min

    Two reports land on your desk in the same week. Both say "landslide blocked the road." One is a hillside that crept forward two metres over a fortnight, tilting the poles and cracking the carriageway but leaving it passable. The other arrived in ninety seconds and left forty metres of alignment as boulders in a khola. The same word covered both — and if you procure the same remedy for both, you will waste the money on one and lose the road on the other. "Landslide" is not a diagnosis.

    The idea

    Ground moves in five distinct ways, and the way it moves tells you the velocity, the reach, and the remedy. Naming the mechanism correctly is the whole of the engineering judgment — everything after it is procurement.

    The classification everyone actually uses

    The standard scheme — Varnes, as revised by Cruden and Varnes and refined since — sorts movement by type of movement first and material second. So a description is always two words: rock fall, debris flow, earth slide. Get in the habit of writing both, because the second word decides half the design.

    TypeWhat it doesSpeedWhere you meet it
    FallDetaches and travels through the air. No shear surface at allExtremely fastSteep rock cuts, undercut cliffs, the Siwaliks and any hard-rock gorge section
    ToppleRotates forward about a point near its base — a column of rock pivoting outFastSteeply dipping, closely jointed rock. Often precedes a fall
    SlideMoves as a coherent mass on a distinct shear surface. Rotational on a curved surface in soil; translational on a planar one — a bedding plane, a joint, the soil/rock interfaceSlow to fastThe bread and butter of the Middle Hills. Most road-cut failures
    SpreadA stiff layer breaks up and extends over a softer one beneathSlow, occasionally suddenUncommon in Nepal's hills; matters where a firm crust sits on soft lacustrine or liquefiable ground
    FlowMoves like a fluid. No coherent blocks, no single shear surface — internal shear throughoutVery slow (creep) to extremely fast (debris flow)Gullies and steep channels. The one that reaches furthest and kills most

    Why the name changes the design

    Take the two reports from the top of the page.

    The slide. A coherent mass on a definable surface. That means there is something to analyse — you can find the surface, estimate the geometry, and calculate a factor of safety that means something. It also means the remedies are the ones from session 6: change the geometry, or take the water out. Unloading the head and draining is often enough. A retaining structure at the toe can work, if it is founded below the slip surface. If it is not, you have built an expensive passenger.

    The flow. There is no single surface, so there is no meaningful factor of safety for the mass, and no wall you can afford will stop it. You are no longer in stability analysis; you are in routing. Where does it come from, where does it go, how much of it is there, and can you get your alignment out of the way or pass the material underneath? Debris flow gets its own session next, because it needs a completely different head.

    Falls and topples are different again. There is nothing to stabilise after the event — the block has gone. The engineering is entirely about the source: scaling, bolting, meshing, or accepting the fall and catching it with a ditch, barrier or net. It is the one family where "do nothing to the slope, manage the consequence" is a legitimate, often correct answer.

    The words that come with it

    Two more descriptors travel with the type, and both should appear in any report you sign.

    Activity. Is it active (moving now), dormant (has moved, could again — the conditions are unchanged), or relict (moved under conditions that no longer exist)? Dormant is the dangerous one, because it looks like stable ground. A great deal of the Middle Hills is old landslide debris that has been quiet for a century, and cutting a road across its toe is exactly the thing that wakes it.

    Depth. Roughly: shallow under about 3 m, deep-seated beyond. This matters because it decides whether your remedy has to reach the surface. A 4 m gabion wall on a 15 m deep slide is decoration.

    Complex and composite movements. Real hillsides do not read the table. The most common sequence in Nepal is a slide that becomes a flow: a mass detaches on a surface, disintegrates as it moves, picks up water in the channel below, and arrives at your road as something quite unlike what left the hillside. Classify by what it will be when it reaches you, not by how it started. Your alignment is downstream, not at the crown.

    Judgment

    Before you accept anyone's landslide report — including your own — insist on both words, plus activity and depth. "Shallow active debris slide becoming flow" tells a designer what to do; "landslide" tells them nothing and invites the default answer, which in Nepal is almost always a gabion wall. That wall is right for perhaps a third of what it gets built for. If you change one habit from this session, make it refusing to write the word alone.

    Field assignment

    On your next drive of any hill road, classify every failure you pass — out loud or on paper, two words each. Aim for ten. For each one, note whether the material stayed on the slope or travelled beyond it.

    What you probably found: a large majority of shallow debris slides on cut faces, a handful of rock falls at the gorge sections, and — this is the useful part — that the ones which actually took out the carriageway were mostly the ones whose material travelled. Reach, not volume, is what closes a road.

    Check yourself

    1 · What are the two words in a proper description, and why is one word never enough?

    Type of movement plus material — "rock fall", "debris flow", "earth slide". Type decides the mechanics and the remedy; material decides the strength parameters, the reach and the construction method. One word without the other leaves the designer guessing at half the problem.

    2 · Why can you calculate a meaningful factor of safety for a slide but not for a flow?

    A slide moves as a coherent mass on a definable shear surface, so there is a real surface on which to compare resisting and driving forces. A flow shears internally throughout, with no single surface — the problem is one of routing and volume, not of limit equilibrium.

    3 · A 4 m gabion wall is proposed at the toe of a slide whose surface is 15 m deep. What is your objection, in one sentence?

    The wall is founded above the slip surface, so the slide will simply carry it along — it adds cost and weight without intersecting the mechanism. Any toe structure must be founded below the surface it is meant to resist.

    4 · Which activity state is the most dangerous to a road engineer, and why?

    Dormant. It has moved before and the conditions that caused it have not changed, but it looks like ordinary stable ground. Cutting a road through the toe of a dormant slide is one of the most reliable ways to reactivate it.

    5 · The most common Nepali sequence is a slide that becomes a flow. Which one do you design your alignment against?

    The flow — because that is what arrives at the road. Classify by what the movement will be when it reaches you, not by how it began at the crown.

    8 · Debris flow

    Session 8 · core · 15 min

    On 15 June 2021 the Melamchi Khola came down in a way nobody on the bazaar's main street had budgeted for. It was not a flood of water. It was a moving mass of water, boulders and forest, and it rebuilt the valley floor as it passed — burying parts of Melamchi Bazar, taking bridges, and damaging the headworks of the water supply scheme that Kathmandu had waited decades for. The rainfall that day was not extraordinary by Nepali standards. What arrived was not proportional to what fell — and understanding why is the difference between a bridge that survives the century and one that does not see out its first monsoon.

    The idea

    A debris flow is not a big flood. It is a different material with different physics — denser, far more destructive per unit of discharge, and capable of carrying boulders that clear water could never move. Size a structure for the flood and you have sized it for the wrong event.

    What makes it a different animal

    Clear water in a channel carries sediment because the flow is turbulent enough to hold it. A debris flow is the other way round: the sediment is the flow, and water is what makes it mobile. Solids typically make up more than half the volume. Three consequences follow, and each one breaks an assumption you carry from hydraulics.

    • Density. Around 1.8–2.3 t/m³ against water's 1.0. Impact pressure on a pier scales with density and with the square of velocity — so the same velocity delivers roughly twice the force, before you count a single boulder.
    • It does not behave as a Newtonian fluid. It has a yield strength. It can stop dead on a slope that clear water would run straight down, sit there through the dry season, and be remobilised by the next event. That is why deposits accumulate on fans instead of washing through.
    • The front. A debris flow arrives as a steep, boulder-rich snout — the coarsest material migrates forward — followed by a more watery tail. The snout does the damage. Peak discharge in the surge can be several times the water-flood peak for the same catchment.

    Entrainment: why it grows on the way down

    This is the mechanism that most surprises people, and the one that decides your design volume. A debris flow travelling down a channel scours the bed and undercuts the banks, and everything it removes joins the flow. A modest initiation — a single shallow slide in a steep headwater — can arrive at the fan as several times its original volume, having eaten the loose material stored in the channel on its way past.

    Which gives you the practical question to ask about any gully crossing your alignment: not how much can detach at the top, but how much loose material is lying in the channel between there and here? A gully that has not run for thirty years is not a safe gully. It is a loaded gully — three decades of rockfall, bank collapse and landslide debris sitting in the bed waiting for a mobilising event. The quiet ones are the dangerous ones.

    Reading a fan before you cross it

    The single most useful skill here is recognising that you are standing on a debris fan at all — because a fan in the dry season is flat, well-drained, gently sloping, easy to build on, and often already settled. It is the most attractive ground in the valley and it is attractive for exactly the reason that should worry you: it was built by the events you are designing against.

    What you seeWhat it tells you
    A cone of ground splaying out where a steep tributary meets a main valleyYou are on a fan. Everything under your feet was delivered by the process
    Boulders far larger than the present stream could ever movePast flows. Their size is a rough gauge of the magnitude you must design for
    The stream sitting in a channel above the surrounding fan surfaceAggrading and prone to avulsion — it will jump its channel and take a new path across the fan, quite possibly through your alignment
    Multiple abandoned channels across the fan surfaceIt has jumped before. The active channel today is not where the next one will be
    Fresh, unweathered, unvegetated depositRecent activity — years, not centuries

    What to do about it

    In rough order of how well it works:

    1. Avoid the fan. Cross the tributary above the fan apex where the channel is confined and you know where the flow will be, or below the fan toe. Costs more in alignment; costs far less over fifty years. This is a session 20 decision, and it is the one that actually matters.
    2. Let it pass. If you must cross the fan, design the crossing to convey the flow rather than resist it — generous clear span, high soffit, no piers in the channel if you can avoid them, and approaches that can be rebuilt cheaply. A causeway or vented ford that the flow passes over may outlast a bridge that it hits.
    3. Control the sediment. Check dams and debris basins upstream reduce what arrives, but they fill. They are a maintenance commitment, not a structure. If nobody is funded to clear them, they will be full when you need them.
    4. Resist it. Deflection walls and barriers. Last, and expensive, and they must be designed for the impact of the snout plus boulder strike — not for hydrostatic pressure.

    The design-life trap. Debris flows on a given fan may recur at intervals of decades. A structure that has stood for thirty years on a fan has not been proven — it may simply not have been tested yet. Do not let "it has always been fine here" enter a design decision. On a fan, the absence of an event is evidence about the interval, not about the safety.

    Judgment

    When your alignment crosses a steep tributary, stop and establish two things before anything else: am I on a fan, and how much loose material is stored in the channel above me. If the answers are yes and a lot, no amount of structural design at the crossing will fix a bad alignment decision — go back and look at crossing above the apex. And when you cannot avoid it, design to let the event through rather than to hold it. The cheapest debris flow structure is the one the flow goes over.

    Field assignment

    Find a place where a steep side stream joins a main valley near a road you know — from memory, or on satellite imagery if you cannot get there. Then:

    1. Trace the fan outline. How far does the splay extend, and where does the road cross it?
    2. Look for boulders in or beside the channel that today's stream plainly could not move.
    3. Look for old channel scars across the fan surface — count them.

    What you probably found: settlement on the fan, the road crossing near the middle of it rather than at the apex, and at least one abandoned channel. That combination — buildings, a mid-fan crossing, and evidence of avulsion — is the standard Nepali valley-mouth situation, and it is why these events produce casualty figures out of all proportion to their size.

    Check yourself

    1 · Why does a debris flow deliver roughly twice the impact force of clear water at the same velocity, before boulders are counted?

    Because impact pressure scales with density, and a debris flow runs at roughly 1.8–2.3 t/m³ against water's 1.0. Velocity squared does the rest — and the boulder strike is an additional, separate load case.

    2 · A gully above your alignment has not run in thirty years. Is that reassuring?

    No — it is the opposite. Thirty quiet years means thirty years of rockfall, bank collapse and slide debris accumulating in the channel bed. It is a loaded gully. The stored material is what a future flow will entrain, and it sets the volume that arrives at your road.

    3 · You find the stream perched in a channel above the general fan surface. What does that predict?

    Avulsion. The channel is aggrading, so it will eventually jump its banks and take a new course across the fan. Today's channel position is not a reliable guide to where the next flow will go — which means a crossing designed only for the present channel is designed for the wrong place.

    4 · Rank avoidance, conveyance, sediment control and resistance for a fan crossing, and say why the order is that way.

    Avoid, convey, control, resist. Avoidance removes the problem instead of managing it; conveyance lets the event pass without asking a structure to survive impact; sediment control works but fills and so is a permanent maintenance commitment; resistance is the most expensive and must be designed for snout impact plus boulder strike. Each step down costs more and delivers less.

    5 · What is wrong with "this crossing has stood for thirty years, so it is fine"?

    On a fan, recurrence intervals run to decades. Thirty years without an event says something about the interval, not about the structure's capacity. It has not been proven — it has not yet been tested.

    9 · Reading a hillside

    Session 9 · core · 14 min

    A hillside that is about to fail almost always says so first. Not in instruments — in cracks, water and the way things lean. The signs are there for months, sometimes years, and they are free to read. What stops most engineers seeing them is not knowledge but habit: we arrive at a site to inspect the structure, and the structure is the one thing on the hillside that will not tell us anything. The ground around it is doing the talking.

    The idea

    A moving slope leaves a signature you can read on foot, in an hour, with no equipment. Learn the six signs and the order to look for them, and you will identify most problems a season before they close the road.

    Where to stand

    Start from across the valley, not on the slope. Distance is the single most underrated instrument in this business — a slide that is invisible when you are standing in it is obvious from the opposite ridge. Look for the shape first: an amphitheatre in the hillside, a bulge in the mid-slope, ground that is hummocky where its neighbours are smooth, or a patch of different vegetation. Then walk it, and walk it from the top down — the crown tells you more than the toe, and it is the part everyone skips because the road is at the bottom.

    The six signs

    1 · Tension cracks at the crown. The first and most reliable sign. Arcuate cracks, roughly parallel to the contour, opening at the head of the moving mass. Measure the width and mark the date on a peg — a crack that is 20 mm wider next month is a slide with a velocity, which is a different object entirely from a crack. Crucially, a crown crack is also a drain: it delivers surface water straight to the slip surface, which is the mechanism from session 6. Sealing crown cracks is one of the highest value-per-rupee interventions in the whole subject.

    2 · The scarp. A step in the ground where the mass has dropped away from what stayed put. Fresh scarps are bare and sharp-edged; older ones round off and grow vegetation. The height of the scarp is roughly the displacement so far.

    3 · Things that lean. Trees, poles, walls, terrace risers. Read them carefully, because the direction tells you the mechanism:

    • Trees tilting backwards into the slope — rotational movement. The mass has rotated on a curved surface, so its upper part tips back.
    • Trees tilting downslope, or bent at the base with a straightened upper trunk — translational movement, or creep. The bend is a record: the tree kept growing vertically while the ground moved under it.

    4 · Disturbed ground surface. Hummocky, irregular ground where the adjacent slope is smooth. Terrace risers that have lost their line. Field boundaries that step. Irrigation channels that have gone out of grade — farmers notice this long before engineers do, which is the subject of the assignment below.

    5 · Water where it was not. The most diagnostic sign of the six. Springs that have appeared, moved or dried up; damp ground that survives the dry season; a line of greener, ranker vegetation across a face. Movement disrupts the drainage paths inside the slope, so a spring that has changed is a slope that has moved. Note the elevation of any seepage line — it is a free measurement of where the water table meets the face.

    6 · The bulging toe. Ground heaving, buckling or overriding at the bottom of the mass. Your road, if it is at the toe, will show it as a rising shoulder, a crest that will not stay level after repeated patching, a retaining wall leaning outward, or a drain that has lost its fall. A drain that keeps silting for no obvious reason is often a drain that is no longer level.

    Distinguishing the real thing from a scar

    Not every bare patch is an active problem. Three questions sort it quickly:

    AskActiveOld / relict
    Are the edges sharp?Sharp, bare, unweatheredRounded, softened, vegetated
    Is the vegetation disturbed?Trees tilted, roots exposed, fresh breaksMature growth standing straight on the deposit
    Do the man-made lines still run true?Terraces, walls, channels, road crest offsetEverything straight, however old

    That third row is the one to lean on. Human beings build straight lines and level channels; the ground does not. Anything straight that has stopped being straight is a displacement gauge somebody else installed for you, for free, years ago.

    One walk is a photograph, not a film. Every sign here tells you a slope has moved. None of them, on a single visit, tells you whether it is moving now or how fast. That needs a second visit and a datum — painted marks across a crack, a peg line, a photograph from a fixed point with a fixed reference in frame. Five minutes on the first visit buys you a real answer on the second. Do it even when you are sure it is dormant.

    Judgment

    Give yourself one rule: never inspect a slope problem from the road alone. The road is at the toe, and the toe is the last part to speak. Walk to the crown. If you do nothing else, seal what cracks you find there and mark them with a date — you will have both reduced the driving mechanism and started a measurement, in an afternoon, for the price of nothing. And ask the farmers. They have watched this hillside for forty years and they will tell you exactly where the spring moved to.

    Field assignment

    Pick a slope above or below a road you use — one you are mildly suspicious of. Walk it from the crown down and record the six signs: present, absent, or uncertain. Then do the two things that make the visit worth repeating:

    1. Paint a mark either side of any crack you find, note the gap in millimetres and write the date on the rock.
    2. Photograph the slope from a spot you can find again, with something permanent in the frame.

    Then find someone who farms there and ask one question: has any spring here changed in your lifetime?

    What you probably found: that the farmer's answer was more precise than anything you measured, and that the signs cluster — where you find one, you find four. Slopes rarely give you a single ambiguous hint. When you genuinely can only find one sign, it usually is nothing.

    Check yourself

    1 · Why is a crown crack both a symptom and a cause?

    It is a symptom because it marks the head of a mass that has already moved. It is a cause because it is an open drain, delivering surface water directly down to the slip surface — raising pore pressure and lowering the factor of safety. Sealing it treats the mechanism, not just the sign.

    2 · Trees on a slope are tilting backwards, into the hill. What does that tell you?

    Rotational movement — the mass has rotated on a curved slip surface, so its upper portion tips back towards the slope. Downslope tilt, or a bent base with a straight upper trunk, indicates translational movement or creep instead.

    3 · A roadside drain keeps silting up and nobody can say why. What should you check?

    Whether it is still level. Toe heave from a slope moving beneath the road can take a drain out of grade, and it will then silt indefinitely no matter how often it is cleaned. The silting is a displacement symptom, not a maintenance failure.

    4 · Which single sign is the most diagnostic of movement, and why?

    Changed water — a spring that has appeared, moved or dried, or seepage that survives the dry season. Movement disrupts the internal drainage paths of a slope, so changed water is direct evidence that the mass has deformed, not merely that it once did.

    5 · What is the one thing a single site visit cannot tell you, and what does five minutes of work buy you?

    It cannot tell you the rate — whether the slope is moving now, and how fast. Painting a datum across a crack, recording the gap with a date, and taking a photo from a findable fixed point converts your next visit into a real measurement of velocity.

    10 · The road as the cause

    Session 10 · core · 16 min

    Fly over the Middle Hills and you can date the roads from the air. A track cut ten years ago is a thin brown line with a fan of bare ground spilling below it, and another one below that, and a gully forming where there was none. The hillside had stood for ten thousand years. Then it was cut, and within a decade it is coming apart — not because the monsoon changed, but because we changed the slope, and then we put the water in the wrong place. This is the most uncomfortable session in the book, and the most useful, because everything in it is within your gift.

    The idea

    Most landslides along Nepal's hill roads were manufactured by the road. Four mechanisms do nearly all of it — the cut, the spoil, the concentrated water and the missing maintenance — and every one of them is a decision somebody made, not a natural hazard that befell them.

    Mechanism 1 — the cut

    A cut removes support from the toe of everything above it. That is not a side effect; it is what a cut is. In session 6 terms you have reduced the resisting force while leaving the driving force untouched, so the factor of safety falls immediately and permanently. The slope may absorb it. On steep ground in weathered material, often it does not.

    Two aggravations turn a survivable cut into a failing one:

    • Cutting too steep for the material. A face is stood up at the angle the excavator can achieve rather than the angle the ground can hold. It survives the dry season on matric suction — the false strength from session 6 — and fails in the first heavy monsoon. The face was never stable; it was only unsaturated.
    • Cutting across the dip. Where bedding, foliation or a major joint set dips out of the face, a cut daylights those surfaces and creates a ready-made translational failure. The same cut on the same hill, driven the other way, would be sound. This is session 11's subject and it is worth knowing that the difference between a stable and an unstable rock cut is often just which side of the valley you are on.

    Mechanism 2 — the spoil

    This is the one that does the most damage and gets the least attention. Excavated material has to go somewhere, and the cheapest place is over the edge.

    Tipped spoil is loose, uncompacted, poorly graded material sitting at or beyond its angle of repose, on a slope that was already at equilibrium. It does three things at once:

    1. Loads the slope below — driving force up, on ground that had no spare capacity.
    2. Buries the natural drainage — the small channels and seepage exits that were quietly relieving pore pressure are smothered, so water backs up inside the slope.
    3. Provides the ammunition for a debris flow — it is loose material stored on a steep slope, directly above a channel. Session 8's loaded gully, delivered to order.

    The results are the fans of bare ground below every dozer-cut track. The road took a metre of hillside and gave back a hundred metres of instability beneath it. And the failure usually appears below the road, which is why it gets recorded as a natural landslide and not as a construction defect.

    Mechanism 3 — concentrated water

    Before the road, rain fell across the hillside and infiltrated or ran off diffusely — a thin sheet over a wide front, at low velocity, doing very little.

    A road is a collector. An impermeable or compacted surface plus a side drain gathers the runoff from a wide catchment and delivers it, concentrated and accelerated, to whichever point the drain happens to end. Nothing about that point was designed to receive it. So one of three things happens:

    • The water is discharged straight onto the slope below and cuts a gully, which lengthens headward until it reaches the road and takes the shoulder.
    • The drain is unlined, so it leaks along its whole length and delivers the water into the slope rather than across it — the pore pressure mechanism from session 6, installed deliberately.
    • The drain silts or blocks, overtops, and the water runs down the carriageway to find the lowest point, usually a fill section or a hairpin, where it does the most damage.

    Look for this on any road you know: the failures are not spread evenly along it. They cluster at drain outlets, hairpins and fill sections — precisely the points where the road decides where water goes. That clustering is the fingerprint of a manufactured problem. A natural hazard would not know where your outlets are.

    Mechanism 4 — the road that is never maintained

    All of the above is survivable if somebody clears the drains before each monsoon. The distinctive Nepali failure of the last two decades is not bad design so much as construction without any provision for maintenance — thousands of kilometres of local track opened by dozer, often without survey, drainage or bio-engineering, and with no budget line for the annual work that would keep them alive.

    The pattern repeats: the track opens, the community gets access, the first monsoon takes sections of it, it is re-dozed, more spoil goes over the side, and the hillside degrades a step further each year. This sits behind the whole logic of the Green Roads approach in session 25, and it is worth saying plainly: the alternative to a well-built road is usually not "no road" — it is a badly built road that will be re-cut every year. Arguing for the standard is arguing for the cheaper option over any horizon longer than three years.

    Say the uncomfortable part. When a slide closes a hill road, the report usually records rainfall. Rainfall was the trigger. If the cut was oversteepened, the spoil went over the edge and the drain discharged onto the slope, then the cause was the road, and the same rain would have done nothing to the untouched hillside beside it. It is worth being the person in the room who says so — not to assign blame, but because if the cause is recorded as rainfall, the reconstruction will repeat the mistake.

    Judgment

    On any hill road you are responsible for, apply three tests before anything else. Where does the spoil go? — if the answer is "over the side", you have a debris source and a loaded slope, and no wall further down will fix it. Where does every drain end? — walk to the outlet, physically, and see what receives the water; an outlet discharging onto open hillside is a gully that has not started yet. Who clears these before the monsoon, and are they paid? — if there is no answer, your drainage design is a drawing, not a system. These three questions will find more risk in an afternoon than a week of stability analysis.

    Field assignment

    Take one kilometre of hill road you know well. Walk it, or drive it slowly, and mark on a rough sketch:

    1. Every drain outlet — and for each, what receives the water. Lined chute? Natural channel? Or open hillside?
    2. Every place spoil has been pushed over the edge.
    3. Every existing failure, above and below the road.

    Now overlay them. How many of the failures sit within fifty metres downhill of an outlet or a spoil tip?

    What you probably found: most of them. That correlation is the single most persuasive thing you can put in front of a client or a ward chair, because it is their own road, drawn by you, in an afternoon — and it turns an argument about the weather into an argument about three specific outlets.

    Check yourself

    1 · In factor-of-safety terms, what does a cut do — and why is it permanent?

    It removes toe support, cutting the resisting force while the driving force is unchanged, so the factor of safety drops immediately. It is permanent because the material is gone: unless you replace that support with a structure or a flatter geometry, the slope stays at the reduced factor of safety for the rest of its life.

    2 · Name the three separate harms done by tipping spoil over the edge.

    It loads the slope below, increasing driving force on ground with no spare capacity. It buries the natural drainage paths that were relieving pore pressure. And it stockpiles loose material on a steep slope above a channel — ready ammunition for a debris flow.

    3 · Why do failures cluster at drain outlets, hairpins and fill sections rather than spreading evenly along a road?

    Because those are the points where the road concentrates and discharges water. A natural hazard would have no reason to prefer them. The clustering is the fingerprint of a road-manufactured problem, not a rainfall-driven one.

    4 · A cut face has stood vertical through two dry seasons in silty material. Why is that not evidence of stability, and what happens in the monsoon?

    It is standing on matric suction — apparent cohesion from partly saturated pores — not on real strength. Saturation removes it completely, and the face fails. The cut was oversteepened from the day it was made; the dry seasons simply hid it.

    5 · A report attributes a road closure to heavy rainfall. When is that description wrong, and why does it matter?

    It is wrong whenever the untouched hillside beside the road survived the same rain. Then rainfall was the trigger and the road — the cut, the spoil, the outlet — was the cause. It matters because a closure recorded as rainfall gets rebuilt exactly as it was, and fails again.

    11 · Rock, not soil

    Session 11 · core · 15 min

    Two cuts, same rock, same height, same contractor, on opposite sides of the same valley. One has stood for twenty years without a stone coming down. The other sheds blocks every monsoon and has swallowed three rounds of remedial work. Nothing in a soil mechanics textbook explains the difference, because the difference is not in the rock. It is in the direction the joints dip, relative to the way you cut the face. Rock does not fail because it is weak. It fails because it is divided — and the geometry of those divisions is the entire subject.

    The idea

    In a rock cut, the strength of the rock is rarely the problem. What governs is the orientation of the discontinuities — bedding, joints, foliation — relative to the face. Get the geometry right and a weak rock will stand; get it wrong and a strong one will not.

    The rock mass, not the rock

    A hand sample tells you the strength of intact rock. A cut face is not intact rock — it is a rock mass: blocks separated by surfaces that have little or no tensile strength and often much lower friction than the rock itself. What matters about those surfaces is five things, and you can assess all five with a compass, a tape and an hour:

    PropertyWhy it matters
    Orientation — dip and dip directionDecides whether a block can move at all. The single most important property
    SpacingSets the block size, which decides whether you are managing boulders or gravel
    Persistence — how far a joint continuesA short joint cannot form a whole failure surface; a persistent one can
    Roughness & infillGoverns friction on the surface. A clay-filled joint may have a friction angle far below the rock's
    WaterPressure in a joint pushes blocks apart and out. Same mechanism as session 6, acting on a plane instead of a slip circle

    The three failure modes, and the one question that finds them

    Before any calculation, ask one kinematic question — can the block physically get out? If the geometry does not permit movement, the mode is impossible regardless of strength.

    Planar sliding. A block slides on a single surface. It needs the surface to dip out of the face, in roughly the same direction as the face (conventionally within about 20°), and to dip less steeply than the face so that it daylights, but more steeply than its own friction angle so that it wants to move. Those three conditions together are the reason a cut on one side of a valley is fine and the same cut opposite is not.

    Wedge sliding. Two joint sets intersect, and the wedge slides along the line of intersection. The test is the same as planar, applied to that line rather than to a plane. Wedges are common and easy to miss, because neither joint set on its own looks threatening — you have to think about where they cross.

    Toppling. Steeply dipping joints running into the face create slender columns that rotate forward about their base. It is the mode people most often misread, because the joints dip into the hill, which looks favourable at a glance. Toppling frequently precedes a fall — a column rotates for years, then goes.

    Stereonets, honestly. The formal tool is a stereographic projection, and if you have several joint sets and a curving alignment, use it — it will find combinations that inspection misses. But you do not need one to make the important decision. Standing at the face with a compass-clinometer, asking does anything dip out of this face, at less than the face angle and more than about thirty degrees, will catch the great majority of problems. The stereonet refines an answer; it does not replace looking.

    What actually holds a rock cut up

    The remedies split by whether you are treating the source or the consequence — and unlike soil slopes, treating the consequence is often the correct engineering answer.

    • Change the geometry. Flatten, or better, re-orient. A minor alignment shift that changes the face direction relative to the joints can be worth more than any amount of bolting. Cheapest at design stage, impossible afterwards — which is the argument for doing the geological mapping before the alignment is fixed, not after.
    • Bench. Berms break the face into shorter heights, catch falling material, and give access for maintenance. Keep them wide enough to actually catch something and to stand on; a 600 mm berm is a drawing convention, not a catch bench.
    • Drain. Water pressure in joints is a driving force. Weep holes and horizontal drains relieve it. Frequently omitted, because a rock face looks like it does not need drainage.
    • Reinforce. Rock bolts and dowels tie blocks back to intact mass; anchors do the same at larger scale. They must cross the discontinuity and be long enough to reach beyond it — a bolt that terminates inside the moving block does nothing.
    • Retain the surface. Mesh, shotcrete. Note the trade-off: shotcrete stops small blocks and ravelling, but it also seals the face, so unless you provide generous weep holes it can raise the very water pressure that drives the failure.
    • Catch it. A catch ditch at the toe, a barrier or a net. For falls especially, this is often the honest answer — cheaper, maintainable, and it does not pretend to have fixed a face that will keep shedding blocks for the life of the road.
    Judgment

    Take a compass-clinometer to every rock cut you inspect and measure three or four joints. It takes ten minutes and it is the difference between an opinion and an assessment. Then ask the design-stage question that nobody asks late enough to matter: can the alignment be shifted or the face re-oriented — because on rock, geometry is bought once and bolts are bought forever. And when a face will keep shedding regardless, say so and design the catch ditch properly. There is no shame in managing a fall you cannot prevent; the shame is in spending the budget pretending to prevent it.

    Field assignment

    Find a rock cut on a road you use — one with a visible joint pattern. From a safe distance:

    1. Sketch the face, and draw on the main discontinuity sets as you see them.
    2. For each set, judge whether it dips out of the face or into it.
    3. Decide which of the three modes — planar, wedge, topple — the geometry permits, and whether the debris on the ground agrees with you.

    What you probably found: that the talus at the toe told you the answer before your sketch did. Block size and shape are a record of the mechanism — slabs point to planar sliding, roughly triangular blocks to wedges, and long columnar pieces to toppling. The ground has already run the analysis; you are checking its working.

    Check yourself

    1 · Two identical cuts in the same rock, opposite sides of a valley. One is stable, one is not. What is the most likely reason?

    Joint or bedding orientation relative to the face. On one side the discontinuities dip into the hill and cannot release a block; on the other they dip out of the face and daylight in it. Same rock, opposite kinematics.

    2 · State the three conditions for planar sliding.

    The surface must dip out of the face and roughly parallel to it (within about 20°); it must dip less steeply than the face, so that it daylights; and it must dip more steeply than its own friction angle, so that sliding is driven. All three, or there is no planar failure.

    3 · Why is toppling the mode most often misread?

    Because its joints dip into the face, which is the classic signature of a favourable geometry. But where they are steep and closely spaced they form slender columns that rotate forward about their base. It looks safe at a glance and often precedes a fall.

    4 · A rock bolt is installed but the block still moves. Give the most likely design error.

    The bolt did not extend far enough beyond the discontinuity into intact rock, so it was anchored within the moving block. A bolt only works if it crosses the surface and develops its anchorage in ground that is staying put.

    5 · Why can shotcreting a face sometimes make things worse?

    It seals the face and prevents joint water from draining, raising the water pressure that drives block movement. It controls ravelling and small blocks but must be combined with generous weep holes or horizontal drains, or it converts a surface problem into a pressure problem.

    12 · Susceptibility on a map

    Session 12 · 13 min

    Everything in this act so far has been an engineer's eye on one slope. But you cannot walk a district, and the people who decide where the road goes, where the school is built and where the settlement expands are not going to walk it either. They need it on a map. The trouble is that a landslide susceptibility map is the most confidently wrong document in the whole field: it looks authoritative, it is coloured red and green, and it is routinely used to answer a question it was never capable of answering.

    The idea

    Susceptibility, hazard and risk are three different maps answering three different questions. Most maps in circulation are the first, most decisions need the third, and almost nobody says which one they are holding.

    The three questions

    MapAnswersIncludes
    SusceptibilityWhere can it happen?Terrain only — slope, geology, soil, land cover, past failures. No time, no consequence
    HazardWhere, how big, how often?Susceptibility plus magnitude and probability — the return period
    RiskWhat does it cost us?Hazard plus what is exposed and how vulnerable it is

    The distinction is not academic. A steep, weak, badly drained hillside with nothing on it is highly susceptible and carries no risk at all. A gentler slope carrying the district's only hospital access may be moderately susceptible and be the most important thing on the map. If a ward chair asks "where should we not build?", susceptibility is a reasonable first answer. If a donor asks "where should we spend first?", susceptibility alone will send the money to the wrong valley.

    How a susceptibility map is actually made

    Whatever the software, every method does the same two things: assemble the factors that control instability, then decide how much each one counts.

    The factors are the ones this act has been about — slope angle, aspect, geology and rock type, structural orientation, soil depth, land cover, distance to drainage, distance to faults and, decisively in Nepal, distance to roads. That last one is not a proxy for something else. It is session 10 showing up as a statistical variable.

    The weighting falls into three families:

    • Knowledge-driven — an experienced geologist assigns the weights. Fast, cheap, works in data-poor districts, and stands or falls entirely on who did it.
    • Data-driven — statistics against an inventory of past landslides: which combinations of factors did failures actually occur on? Objective, and only as good as the inventory.
    • Physically based — an actual slope-stability calculation on a grid, coupled to a groundwater model. Most rigorous, hungriest for data, and realistically limited to small, well-characterised areas.

    All three depend on the same foundation: an inventory of past landslides. Without it the data-driven methods have nothing to learn from and the others have nothing to be checked against. The single most valuable thing a district engineer can contribute to this work is not a model — it is a properly located, properly dated record of what has already failed.

    What these maps cannot do

    Four limits, and you should be able to state them to a client without notes:

    1. They do not tell you when. A susceptibility map has no time axis at all. "High susceptibility" does not mean "soon"; it means "if the trigger comes, here is where".
    2. They are only as honest as the inventory. Inventories are usually built from imagery and from reported events — so they over-record failures near roads and settlements, where people are looking, and under-record remote ones. Feed that into a statistical model and you get a map that has learned where people live as much as where slopes fail.
    3. The scale is not negotiable. A map made at 1:50,000 has grid cells tens of metres across and cannot say anything about your specific cut. Using a regional map to clear an individual site is the most common misuse in circulation, and it is exactly the misuse that gets an engineer's name onto a decision they never actually made.
    4. They go out of date, and Nepal's go out of date fast. Every new road changes the input layer. A map made before a track was cut across a hillside is describing a hillside that no longer exists.

    The colour problem. A map with red zones invites people to read a boundary as a line between danger and safety. There is no such line — susceptibility is continuous, the class breaks were chosen by somebody, and a cell just inside "moderate" is indistinguishable from the cell next to it in "high". When you present one of these, say that out loud. If you do not, the map will be treated as a permit.

    Using it well

    Used honestly, these maps are genuinely powerful, and the power is in screening — not in deciding. Three good uses:

    • Corridor selection. Comparing two or three alignment options across a district, before anybody surveys anything. This is the highest-value use by a distance, because it is the stage where the decision is still cheap. Session 20's subject.
    • Prioritising the walking. You cannot inspect four hundred kilometres. You can inspect the forty that the map flags — which turns a hopeless task into a week's work.
    • Land use planning. Keeping new settlement and new public buildings off the worst ground is the cheapest risk reduction available to anyone, and it needs only a susceptibility map and the will to apply it. Session 32 returns to why the will is the hard part.

    And one rule that keeps you out of trouble: a map narrows where you walk; it never replaces walking. Every site-specific decision needs somebody on the slope with the eye from session 9.

    Judgment

    When a susceptibility map is put in front of you, ask three questions before you look at the colours. What scale was it made at — and is it being used at that scale? What inventory is it built on — how complete, how old, and does it include the roads that were cut since? Is this susceptibility, hazard or risk — because the answer decides what question it can settle. If you cannot get those three answers, you have a picture, not a map. And when you present one, name its limits in the same breath as its findings; the alternative is that somebody treats your red line as a building permit boundary.

    Field assignment

    Find any hazard or susceptibility map covering ground you know well — a district profile, a project report, a portal, anything to hand. Then:

    1. Find three places you personally know have failed. What class does the map put them in?
    2. Find one place the map calls high susceptibility that you know has been stable in living memory. Why might the map think otherwise?
    3. Check whether roads built in the last ten years appear in the input data at all.

    What you probably found: broadly sensible agreement at the regional scale, and at least one confident disagreement at the site scale — which is the lesson. These maps are right about districts and unreliable about slopes. Your local knowledge is worth more than the map at the scale you actually work.

    Check yourself

    1 · A hillside is steep, weak, badly drained and completely uninhabited. High susceptibility or high risk?

    High susceptibility, no meaningful risk. Risk requires exposure — something of value that can be harmed. Susceptibility describes the terrain alone; it says nothing about consequence and nothing about timing.

    2 · Why does "distance to roads" appear as a factor in Nepali susceptibility models?

    Because roads cause landslides — the cut, the tipped spoil and the concentrated drainage from session 10. It is not a proxy for terrain; the statistics are picking up a real causal mechanism that human construction introduced.

    3 · How can an incomplete landslide inventory bias a data-driven map, and in which direction?

    Inventories are compiled from imagery and reported events, so they over-record failures near roads and settlements where people are looking, and under-record remote ones. A model trained on that partly learns where people live rather than where slopes fail — inflating apparent susceptibility near infrastructure.

    4 · What is the most common misuse of a regional susceptibility map?

    Using a map made at, say, 1:50,000 to clear or condemn an individual site. Its cells are tens of metres across and it carries no information at that resolution. It can narrow where you walk; it cannot substitute for a site inspection.

    5 · Someone points at the boundary of a red zone and asks whether the land just outside it is safe to build on. What do you say?

    That there is no such boundary in reality — susceptibility is continuous and the class breaks were a human choice, so a cell just outside "high" is indistinguishable from one just inside. The map screens; it does not permit. That site needs someone to walk it.

    13 · The catchment

    Session 13 · core · 14 min

    The bridge over the main river is still there. It is the culvert eight kilometres back, on a khola nobody has ever named, that took the road out. This is the ordinary pattern on hill roads, and it offends the intuition — the big river gets the engineering, the survey, the hydrological study and the review panel, while the small catchment gets a standard pipe from a schedule of rates. Yet the small steep catchment is the one that behaves violently, and the reason is a single quantity that most cross-drainage design never calculates.

    The idea

    A catchment's response time — how long runoff takes to reach the outlet — decides what storm it is vulnerable to. Small and steep means minutes, and rainfall intensity over minutes is far higher than over hours. Design a fast catchment on a slow storm and you will undersize it every time.

    Time of concentration

    Time of concentration (tc) is the time for runoff from the hydraulically most remote point in the catchment to arrive at the outlet. It is the single most useful number in small-catchment hydrology, because of what follows from it:

    The critical storm is the one whose duration equals tc.

    Shorter than tc and the whole catchment is never contributing at once. Much longer and the average intensity has dropped. The peak arrives when the storm duration and the catchment's response time match.

    The Kirpich relation is the usual quick estimate for small steep catchments:

    tc = 0.0195 · L0.77 · S−0.385

    with tc in minutes, L the flow path length in metres, and S the average slope as a ratio. Note what the exponents are telling you: slope matters disproportionately. Steepen a catchment and tc collapses.

    Run it once for a typical hill khola — say a 900 m flow path falling 300 m, so S = 0.33 — and you get something in the region of ten to fifteen minutes. That is the storm duration your culvert is actually exposed to. Now ask what rainfall intensity is used in most small cross-drainage design in practice, and you will usually find a figure derived from a 24-hour depth. A 24-hour average intensity and a 15-minute intensity are not the same number, and they are not close.

    The rational method, and where it earns its keep

    For small catchments the workhorse is still:

    Q = 0.278 · C · I · A

    Q in m³/s, C the runoff coefficient, I the rainfall intensity in mm/hr for a storm of duration tc, and A the catchment area in km². The method is crude, and it is crude in a way worth understanding: it assumes uniform rainfall over the whole catchment, a constant C, and that the peak occurs when the whole area contributes. Those assumptions hold reasonably for small areas and fail progressively as the catchment grows. Beyond a few square kilometres you should be reaching for a regional method or a unit hydrograph, which is session 14.

    Two of the three inputs are usually wrong in practice, and they are wrong in the same direction:

    • I is taken at the wrong duration. If you use a 24-hour intensity for a fifteen-minute catchment, you have understated the design storm severely. Intensity rises steeply as duration falls — this is the single most common error in hill cross-drainage.
    • C is taken from the land cover on the survey drawing, not the land cover that will exist. Runoff coefficients rise with compaction, deforestation, terracing and — above all — the road itself. Your road adds impermeable or compacted surface and a drainage network to a catchment that had neither. You are part of C.

    What else the catchment's shape is telling you

    PropertyWhat it does to the peak
    AreaSets total volume. Rises roughly linearly; peak discharge per unit area falls as area grows, because a big catchment never receives its design storm all at once
    SlopeShortens tc sharply. The dominant control on hill roads
    ShapeA compact, fan-shaped catchment delivers everything at once — high peak. A long thin one staggers arrivals — lower peak, longer duration
    Drainage densityMore channels per km² means faster collection and a flashier response
    Land coverSets C and modifies tc. Forest slows and absorbs; bare, compacted or burnt ground does neither
    Stored debrisNot a hydrological property, but the one that decides whether what arrives is water or a debris flow. Back to session 8

    The catchment you design for is not the catchment you surveyed. Over the life of a structure the land cover will change, more roads will be cut above it, terraces will be abandoned or extended, and the drainage density will rise. Every one of those pushes C up and tc down — both in the direction of a higher peak. A culvert sized exactly to today's catchment is sized for the most benign conditions it will ever see. This is one of the few places where deliberate generosity is cheap: a larger pipe costs a little more once, and blocks less often forever.

    Judgment

    Before sizing any cross-drainage, spend ten minutes on the catchment above it: pace or measure the flow path, estimate the fall, and get a tc. If it comes out under half an hour — which on hill ground it usually will — then insist that the rainfall intensity used matches that duration. This one habit will change more of your structures than any refinement of the discharge formula. And when you are deciding between two pipe sizes on a steep khola, remember that you are not just sizing for water. You are sizing for water carrying whatever the catchment has been storing, which is why session 22 argues that blockage, not discharge, is the real design case.

    Field assignment

    Pick one culvert on a hill road you know. Standing at it, look up at what drains to it and estimate three things — roughly is fine:

    1. The flow path length from the top of the catchment to the culvert.
    2. The fall over that length, so you have S.
    3. Run Kirpich. What is tc?

    Then ask the harder question: what rainfall duration was that culvert most likely designed on?

    What you probably found: a tc of ten to thirty minutes, and no way of telling what storm the culvert was designed for — because it was almost certainly picked from a standard drawing rather than calculated at all. That is not negligence so much as the normal condition of small cross-drainage everywhere. It is also why so much of it fails.

    Check yourself

    1 · Why is the critical storm duration equal to the time of concentration?

    Shorter than t𝒸 and the whole catchment never contributes simultaneously, so the peak is limited by area. Longer than t𝒸 and the average intensity has fallen. The peak discharge occurs where the two effects cross — when storm duration matches the catchment's response time.

    2 · A designer uses a 24-hour rainfall depth for a catchment with t𝒸 of fifteen minutes. Which way is the error, and is it small?

    It underestimates, and badly. Rainfall intensity rises steeply as duration shortens, so a 15-minute intensity is several times the 24-hour average. The structure is undersized for the storm it is actually exposed to — the commonest error in hill cross-drainage.

    3 · Two catchments have the same area and the same total rainfall, but one is steep and compact and the other long and gentle. Which needs the larger culvert?

    The steep compact one. Volume is the same, but a culvert is sized on peak discharge — and a short t𝒸 with simultaneous arrival from all parts of the catchment produces a much higher, briefer peak.

    4 · In what way is the road itself an input to the rational method?

    It raises the runoff coefficient C by adding compacted or impermeable surface, and it shortens t𝒸 by adding a drainage network that collects and accelerates flow. The engineer is modifying the catchment that the design is based on.

    5 · Why is a structure sized precisely to today's catchment sized optimistically?

    Because land cover degrades, more roads get cut above it and drainage density rises over the structure's life — all of which push C up and t𝒸 down, and both raise the peak. Today's catchment is the most benign version the structure will ever face.

    14 · The number you are judged on

    Session 14 · core · 15 min

    Somewhere in every bridge or culvert design there is a single figure — the design discharge — and everything downstream of it is arithmetic. Waterway, foundation depth, apron size, freeboard, cost. If that number is wrong, no amount of careful detailing recovers the situation. And here is the uncomfortable part: on most Nepali catchments it is an estimate made without gauge data, by a regional method, to a precision the underlying information cannot support. Knowing how wrong it might be is not pessimism. It is what tells you where to put your margin.

    The idea

    Design discharge is a decision about acceptable risk dressed up as a calculation. Choose the return period consciously, know which method you used and what it assumes, and put your margin where the uncertainty actually is — which is almost never in the third significant figure.

    Choosing the return period

    A return period T does not mean "once every T years". It means an annual exceedance probability of 1/T. The consequence people rarely compute:

    P(at least one exceedance in n years) = 1 − (1 − 1/T)n

    Design return periodChance of being exceeded in a 50-year life
    25 years87 %
    50 years64 %
    100 years39 %
    200 years22 %

    Read that table properly, because it reframes the whole exercise. A structure on a 50-year flood is more likely than not to see its design event exceeded within its service life. That is not a design failure — it is the deal you struck. The real question is not "will it be exceeded" but "what happens when it is", and that question deserves an answer in the design, not a silence.

    Typical practice scales the return period with consequence — culverts and minor cross-drainage at the lower end, important bridges at 100 years and sometimes beyond, with the highest values reserved for structures whose loss would sever a district. Take the actual figures from the current DoR bridge and road standards rather than from memory or from the last project, because these values are revised, and an out-of-date return period is the easiest possible thing to be caught on.

    Two adjustments worth arguing for on their merits:

    • Redundancy should shift the number. A crossing with a viable alternative route ten kilometres away is a different risk proposition from the only link to a district headquarters, even if the two bridges are identical. The standard sets a floor, not a ceiling.
    • Check the extreme case separately. Design for the standard event; then ask what a much larger one does. If the answer is "the approaches wash out and we rebuild them in a fortnight", that is an acceptable structure. If it is "the pier is undermined and the deck is in the river", it is not — whatever the design return period says.

    Getting the number when there is no gauge

    Most hill catchments in Nepal are ungauged. In rough order of how much you should trust them:

    1. Frequency analysis on a long local record. Fit Gumbel or log-Pearson III to annual maxima at a nearby gauge and transfer by area ratio. Best when the record is long and the gauge is genuinely on a comparable catchment. Be honest about long: extrapolating a hundred-year flood from a twenty-year record is an extrapolation, not an analysis, and the confidence interval is wider than the answer.
    2. Regional methods. In Nepal the WECS/DHM regional approach is the standard fallback for ungauged basins, estimating flood magnitudes from catchment area and elevation characteristics. Use the current published coefficients — do not carry them in your head — and note that regional methods describe the region, so a catchment that is atypical of its region will be badly served.
    3. Rational method. Session 13. Sound for small catchments, degrades as area grows.
    4. Empirical area formulae of the Dickens or Ryves type. Fast, and genuinely useful only as a sanity check on something else.

    Always use two methods. Not to average them — averaging two estimates of unknown quality produces a third estimate of unknown quality. Use the second one to find out whether you have made an error of a factor of two, which is the error that actually happens. If they agree within a reasonable band, proceed. If one is triple the other, stop and find out why before you pick, because the reason is usually a mistake in catchment area, units, or an intensity taken at the wrong duration.

    Where the uncertainty really lives

    This is the part that separates a competent designer from a careful one. Rank the things that can make your waterway inadequate:

    SourceRough effect on required waterway
    Blockage by debris — trees, boulders, a collapsed bank upstreamCan remove most of the opening. Dominates everything else
    Bed aggradation over the structure's lifeSteadily reduces the opening; on a Himalayan river this is routine, not exceptional
    Choice of return periodTens of per cent
    Choice of estimation methodTens of per cent, sometimes a factor of two
    Refining the coefficients within a methodA few per cent

    The list is in that order for a reason, and the reason is a design instruction: effort spent refining the discharge estimate below its own uncertainty is wasted, while effort spent on blockage, aggradation and freeboard pays directly. A generously sized, well-aligned opening with real freeboard and a maintainable inlet will outperform a precisely calculated one that silts.

    Say what you assumed, in the drawing. Note the design discharge, the return period, the method and the date on the structural drawing itself — not only in a report that will be lost. In fifteen years somebody will be assessing this structure after a flood that exceeded it, and the difference between "the design was inadequate" and "the design was for a 50-year event and this was larger" is a line of text you can write today. It also protects the next engineer from assuming a capacity that was never there.

    Judgment

    Treat the design flood as two questions, not one. First: what event am I designing for, and does the return period match the consequence of losing this crossing — including whether there is another way round? Second, and the one most designs skip: what does this structure do when that event is exceeded? Aim for structures that fail gracefully — approaches that wash out and are rebuilt cheaply, decks that overtop without being lifted, aprons that launch instead of undermining. Then put your remaining margin into opening size and freeboard rather than into decimal places, because the thing that will actually reduce your waterway is a tree.

    Field assignment

    Take one bridge or major culvert you know and reconstruct its design decision:

    1. Estimate the catchment area above it — from a map or imagery is fine.
    2. Estimate a design discharge by two different methods.
    3. Compare them. If they differ by more than about a factor of two, find the reason.
    4. Then look at the structure and ask: what happens if the flood is twice your larger number? Does it overtop and survive, or does it lose a pier?

    What you probably found: that the two methods disagreed by a substantial margin — which is normal and is exactly why you run two — and that the question about graceful failure was the one that changed your opinion of the structure. Most crossings look adequate until you ask what they do on the day they are exceeded.

    Check yourself

    1 · A bridge is designed for the 50-year flood and has a 50-year life. What is the chance the design event is exceeded, and what does that mean?

    About 64 % — from 1 − (1 − 1/50)⁵⁰. It is more likely than not. That is not a design failure but the risk that was accepted, which is precisely why the design must also answer what happens when it is exceeded.

    2 · Why use two estimation methods, if you are not going to average them?

    To detect gross error. Averaging two estimates of unknown quality just gives a third of unknown quality. The second method's job is to reveal a factor-of-two discrepancy — usually caused by a wrong catchment area, a units slip, or an intensity taken at the wrong duration — before it reaches the drawing.

    3 · What is the single largest threat to a waterway's adequacy, and where does refining the discharge estimate rank against it?

    Blockage by debris, which can remove most of the opening — far outweighing any refinement of Q. Bed aggradation comes next. Refining coefficients within a method changes the answer by a few per cent and is the least valuable place to spend effort.

    4 · Two identical bridges, same river, same discharge. One is the only link to a district headquarters; the other has a diversion ten kilometres away. Should they be designed the same?

    No. Return period should scale with the consequence of losing the crossing, and consequence includes redundancy. The standard sets a floor; the absence of an alternative route is a legitimate argument for going above it.

    5 · Why should the design discharge, return period, method and date appear on the drawing itself?

    Because someone will assess the structure after an exceedance, and the difference between "inadequate design" and "designed for a 50-year event, this was larger" is that line of text. It also stops the next engineer assuming a capacity the structure never had.

    15 · Rivers behave

    Session 15 · core · 15 min

    On 18 August 2008 the Koshi breached its eastern embankment near Kusaha, a few kilometres above the barrage, and left. It did not overtop in a record flood — the discharge that day was well below the barrage's capacity. It simply took a lower path across a plain it had spent decades building up, swung tens of kilometres east of its engineered course, and displaced a great many people in Nepal and Bihar who lived nowhere near the river. A structure can be perfectly adequate for the water and still be irrelevant, because the river is no longer there.

    The idea

    A Himalayan river is a sediment conveyor that happens to contain water. Design for the water alone and you will be right about the hydraulics and wrong about the river — because the bed level, the channel position and the plan form all move, and all of them move faster than your structure's design life.

    The sediment, first

    The Himalaya is young, rising, steep and heavily rained on, and it is shedding itself into the plains at a rate among the highest anywhere on earth. Every river issue you will meet follows from that. Sediment travels in three ways, and the distinction is practical:

    • Bed load — rolling, sliding and hopping along the bed. Coarse. It is what builds bars, fills your waterway, and determines whether the bed rises or falls. This is the fraction that matters most to a bridge.
    • Suspended load — held up by turbulence. What makes the river brown. Mostly passes through.
    • Wash load — the finest fraction, effectively never deposited in the channel. Irrelevant to your structure and highly relevant to a reservoir.

    Transport capacity rises very steeply with velocity, which produces the behaviour that catches people out: almost all the year's sediment moves in a few days. The river you survey in March is not doing the work. It is resting between shifts.

    Aggradation and degradation

    Whether the bed rises or falls is a simple balance — sediment supplied from upstream against sediment the river can carry away. Where supply exceeds capacity the bed aggrades; where capacity exceeds supply it degrades.

    Both are engineering problems, and they are opposite ones:

    AggradationDegradation
    Typical causeHeavy upstream sediment yield — landslides, debris flows, a catchment being disturbed. Or a reduction in slope or velocity: the plains, a backwater, a barrageSediment cut off upstream (a dam), or extraction — sand and gravel mining
    What it does to youReduces waterway. Raises flood levels. Buries aprons and drains. Perches the channel above its floodplainLowers the bed beneath your foundations. Undermines piers, aprons, spurs, protection works
    Where in NepalThe rule on most rivers leaving the hills, and pronounced above any barrageIncreasingly common downstream of extraction reaches near urban demand

    Note that unregulated gravel extraction near a bridge causes degradation exactly where you can least afford it, and it is often happening a few hundred metres from a structure whose owner has no idea. It is worth looking for on every site visit.

    Plan form: what the river looks like from above

    Braided. Multiple channels around shifting bars, characteristic of high sediment load, steep slope and erodible banks — which describes most Nepali rivers where they leave the hills. The engineering consequence is blunt: you cannot site a structure on "the channel", because there isn't one. The active thread this year is not the active thread in ten years. You must either span the whole braid belt or accept that you are committing to guide bunds and training works forever.

    Meandering. A single sinuous channel that erodes the outer bank of each bend and deposits on the inner. It migrates continuously — that is not a malfunction, it is what meandering is. Siting an abutment on an outer bend without protection is siting it at the point of attack.

    Avulsion. The one from the top of this session. A river that aggrades eventually perches its bed above the surrounding floodplain, held in by its own levees or by yours. At that point it is a river looking for a lower path, and it will find one — often in a flood well below the design event, because the mechanism is not capacity, it is a breach.

    Reading a river before you design in it

    Almost all of the following is available from imagery and an afternoon, and it is worth more than an extra decimal place on Q:

    • Compare old and recent imagery. Twenty years of images shows you migration rate and direction directly. This is the single most valuable half hour in river work.
    • Map the abandoned channels. They define the belt the river considers its own. Anything inside it is at risk, whatever it looks like today.
    • Look at the banks. Fresh vertical scarps mean active attack. Sloped vegetated banks mean stability, for now.
    • Look at the bed. Compare bed level with old bridge soffits, apron levels or a known datum. Rising or falling, and how fast?
    • Ask about extraction. Where is sand and gravel being taken, and how close to your crossing?

    Confinement moves the problem, it does not remove it. Embankments, guide bunds and spurs constrain a river to a narrower path. That raises velocity and flood level inside the constraint, cuts off the floodplain sediment sink so the bed aggrades faster, and transfers attack downstream to whoever is next. Sometimes it is still the right answer. But it should be chosen knowing that a confined aggrading river is being loaded towards the day it breaks out — which is the Koshi story in a sentence.

    Judgment

    Before fixing any crossing point, put twenty years of imagery side by side and ask where the river has been, not where it is. Then site for the belt, not the channel — span the braid, stand off the outer bend, keep abutments and approaches out of the migration corridor. Where you cannot, be explicit that you have bought a permanent training-works commitment and make sure somebody is funded for it, because unmaintained training works fail faster than no training works. And treat any perched, aggrading, embanked reach as a standing avulsion risk regardless of how well the embankment is built.

    Field assignment

    Pick a river crossing you know and open the oldest and newest satellite imagery you can find of it. Then:

    1. Trace the active channel in each. How far has it moved, and in which direction?
    2. Mark every abandoned channel you can see. Sketch the outer envelope — that is the belt.
    3. Where does the structure and its approach embankment sit relative to that envelope?

    What you probably found: more movement than you expected, and an approach embankment — not the bridge — sitting inside the belt. Approaches are where these rivers actually take roads out. The span gets the design attention and the embankment gets outflanked.

    Check yourself

    1 · The Koshi left its course in 2008 at a discharge well below the barrage's capacity. What does that tell you about the failure mechanism?

    That it was not a capacity failure. Years of aggradation had perched the bed above the surrounding plain, so the river was held in by its embankments and looking for a lower path. It breached rather than overtopped — which is why a structure adequate for the water was irrelevant to the outcome.

    2 · Which sediment fraction matters most to a bridge, and why?

    Bed load. It is coarse, it builds bars, it fills the waterway and it governs whether the bed aggrades or degrades. Suspended load mostly passes through and wash load never deposits in the channel — they matter to a reservoir, not to your foundation.

    3 · Why is a braided river fundamentally different to site a structure on?

    Because there is no single channel to site on — the active thread shifts between floods across the whole braid belt. You either span the belt or you commit permanently to guide bunds and training works to hold the river where you want it.

    4 · Gravel is being extracted 300 m downstream of a bridge. What is the concern?

    Degradation. Removing sediment lowers the bed, and the effect propagates upstream, undermining pier foundations, aprons and protection works. It is a slow, invisible reduction in the scour margin the foundations were designed with.

    5 · Name the trade-off in confining a river with embankments and guide bunds.

    Confinement raises velocity and flood level within the constraint, cuts off the floodplain as a sediment sink so the bed aggrades faster, and passes the attack downstream. It can still be right — but a confined, aggrading river is being loaded steadily towards avulsion.

    16 · Flood, flash flood, GLOF

    Session 16 · core · 14 min

    On 4 August 1985, Dig Tsho — a moraine-dammed lake above Thame in the Khumbu — released. The wave went down the Bhote Koshi and destroyed the hydropower plant at Thame that was within weeks of commissioning, along with bridges, trails and farmland for tens of kilometres. It had not rained. In August 2024 the same valley was hit again, when lakes above Thame burst and ran through the village. A flood is normally something you can see coming in the sky. These are floods with no weather attached — and if your mental model of flooding is "heavy rain, rising river", you will not plan for them at all.

    The idea

    Three different things get called flooding, and they differ in the one variable that decides what you can do about them: warning time. Days, minutes, or none. That difference, not the depth of water, should drive your response.

    The three, sorted by what you get to do

    Read down the "warning" row and the design philosophy writes itself. Where warning is measured in days you can invest in forecasting and evacuation, and a person can be moved out of harm's way. Where it is measured in minutes, no warning system will save a structure and barely one will save a person who is not already close to high ground — so the money belongs in siting rather than in warning.

    Riverine floods

    The familiar one, and the one Nepal's institutional machinery is best set up for. Its distinguishing feature is that it is observable upstream before it arrives, which makes real warning possible. Community-based flood early warning systems on rivers such as the Karnali, Babai and the Rapti systems work on exactly this — an upstream gauge, a simple threshold, and a phone call or siren downstream.

    The honest limitation is worth knowing precisely: the lead time is only the travel time. On Nepal's rivers, from the hills to the Terai settlements, that is typically a few hours. It is enough to move people and livestock to a raised platform. It is not enough to move anything that requires machinery, and it is not enough to protect a structure. Design accordingly — a warning system is a life-safety measure, and it should never be counted as a substitute for freeboard.

    Flash floods

    Session 13's short time of concentration, expressed as a hazard. A steep catchment converts an intense burst into a violent peak within minutes, and in Nepal that peak very often arrives as a debris flow rather than as water — which is session 8.

    The practical consequences for a road engineer:

    • Warning is effectively impossible at the scale of an individual crossing. By the time an upstream observer sees it, it has arrived.
    • The design event is not "the flood" but "the flood plus what it is carrying". A culvert sized on clear-water discharge is sized for a fraction of the real problem.
    • The controllable variable is position — where the alignment crosses, how high, and whether the structure is on the fan or above it.

    Outburst floods: GLOF and LDOF

    Two mechanisms, one behaviour. A natural dam impounds a lake, then fails.

    GLOF — a glacial lake, usually behind a moraine left by a retreating glacier. The moraine is loose, unengineered material, often with buried ice in it, and it can be breached by overtopping, by piping through the dam, or by a wave from an ice or rock avalanche falling into the lake. As glaciers retreat, these lakes generally grow in number and volume, so this hazard is not stationary — it is increasing, which session 19 takes up.

    LDOF — a landslide dam, the mechanism that made Jure so serious in session 6. A slide blocks a river, a lake forms behind it, and the dam is then either breached naturally or by intervention. Landslide dams tend to fail fast, and often within days or weeks of forming, which at least makes the danger period identifiable.

    What makes both far worse than their volume suggests is that the release entrains its own channel. It scours bed and banks as it travels and can arrive downstream several times larger than what left the lake, exactly as in session 8. A GLOF is a debris flow with a reservoir behind it.

    You cannot economically design a road structure for a GLOF. Say this plainly, and early, because a great deal of money gets spent pretending otherwise. The peak discharge and the impact loads are far beyond anything a normal crossing can be built for. What you can do is real, and it is all in the layout: keep the alignment and the structure above the credible inundation and impact zone, keep piers out of the channel, avoid building on fans and outwash terraces below known lakes, and accept that if you must be low, the structure is expendable and should be cheap and quick to replace. Choosing where to be is the only economic defence.

    What to do with this, concretely

    1. Find out what is upstream of you. For any alignment in a glaciated valley, establish whether there are moraine-dammed lakes in the catchment. This information exists — ICIMOD and the department inventories have mapped Nepal's glacial lakes and flagged the potentially dangerous ones — and it should be a routine input to a route decision, not a specialist study nobody commissions.
    2. Look for the evidence on the ground. Past outburst deposits are written into the valley: boulder fields far above the present river, terraces of coarse unsorted material, trim lines on the valley sides. Session 9's eye, applied to a valley floor.
    3. Set your levels from the evidence, not the hydrology. If the deposits say the flow was once eight metres above today's bed, that is your constraint, whatever the frequency analysis says.
    4. Know the danger period after a landslide dam forms. When a slide blocks a river anywhere upstream of your work, that is an immediate operational question — who is downstream, what is the lake volume, and is anyone watching it.
    Judgment

    Classify the crossing before you design it. Ask what kind of flood threatens this point, and then spend where that kind can actually be influenced: forecasting and levels for riverine, siting and conveyance for flash, and for outburst floods, elevation and expendability — because nothing else is affordable. The most common error is to apply a riverine mindset everywhere, sizing a waterway from a frequency curve on a khola whose real design event will be a wall of boulders that no frequency curve describes.

    Field assignment

    Take a valley you know that drains from high country. On imagery, work upstream from a road crossing and answer:

    1. Are there lakes in the catchment? Any behind moraine?
    2. On the valley floor near the crossing, can you find boulders or terraces well above the present river that today's flow could not have placed?
    3. How high above the present bed is the highest such evidence — and how does that compare with the soffit of the structure?

    What you probably found: evidence of past flows above the level anyone designs to, and a structure set by hydrology rather than by that evidence. The valley keeps a longer record than the gauge does, and it is usually the more alarming of the two.

    Check yourself

    1 · Which single variable most usefully separates riverine, flash and outburst floods for design purposes?

    Warning time — days, minutes, or none. It decides whether forecasting and evacuation can work at all, and therefore whether money belongs in warning systems or entirely in siting and levels.

    2 · A community flood warning system gives a few hours of lead time. What can and cannot that achieve?

    It can move people and livestock to safe ground — it is a life-safety measure. It cannot protect a structure, and it cannot move anything needing machinery. Lead time equals the flood's travel time, so it must never be counted as a substitute for freeboard or siting.

    3 · Why is a GLOF's downstream impact often much larger than the lake volume implies?

    Because the release entrains its channel — scouring bed and banks as it travels — so it grows on the way down and arrives as a debris flow rather than as water. It is a debris flow with a reservoir behind it.

    4 · Why is glacial lake outburst hazard described as non-stationary?

    Because retreating glaciers are creating and enlarging moraine-dammed lakes, so the number and volume of potential sources is increasing over time. The historical record understates the future rate — you cannot infer the hazard from past frequency.

    5 · Your alignment must cross a valley below a known dangerous glacial lake. What is the design strategy?

    Elevation and expendability, not strength. Keep the alignment and structure above the credible inundation and impact zone, keep piers out of the channel, avoid fans and outwash terraces — and where you must be low, make the structure cheap and quick to replace. You cannot economically build to resist the event.

    17 · Scour

    Session 17 · core · 16 min

    Ask what takes bridges down in Nepal and most people will say earthquakes. The record says otherwise. Bridges are lost, year after year, to water — and specifically to the ground being removed from under a foundation that was perfectly adequate for every load it was designed to carry. What makes scour dangerous is not that it is difficult. It is that it is invisible. The hole opens during the flood, at night, under brown water nobody can see into, and by the time the river drops it has filled itself back in with loose sediment. You inspect at low water, find a bed that looks exactly as it should, and conclude that nothing happened.

    The idea

    Scour is at its deepest at the peak of the flood and refills as the flood recedes. What you can see at low water tells you almost nothing about what happened. Foundations must be taken below the total scour depth, which is three separate mechanisms added together.

    Three mechanisms, and they add

    1 · General scour — the long-term change in bed level of the whole reach, and the short-term lowering during a flood. It has nothing to do with your bridge. It is session 15's degradation, plus any gravel extraction, plus the natural fluctuation of the bed during high flow. A bridge founded correctly in 1990 can be inadequate in 2025 because the reach has degraded, without a single thing about the bridge having changed.

    2 · Contraction scour — the bed lowering caused by your structure narrowing the waterway. Squeeze the flow and velocity rises; raise velocity and transport capacity rises steeply; more sediment leaves the contracted section than enters it, so the bed drops until the two balance. This one is directly under your control, and it is the cost of a short bridge: every metre of waterway you save at the deck buys you depth you must pay for in the foundations. That trade is worth pricing honestly rather than assuming the shorter span is cheaper.

    3 · Local scour — the hole around a pier or abutment. Flow striking a pier is driven downwards at the face, forming a horseshoe vortex that wraps around the base and excavates the bed. It is a highly localised, aggressive mechanism, and it is the one that most often takes the structure.

    Estimating it

    South Asian practice, including Nepali bridge design, builds on Lacey's regime theory. The regime scour depth is estimated from the discharge intensity and a silt factor representing the bed material, and then multiplied by factors depending on where you are — larger at a pier nose, larger still at a severe bend or a constriction. The IRC bridge codes set out the multipliers.

    Rather than reproduce numbers you should read from the current code, learn the shape of the relationships, because that is what lets you smell a wrong answer:

    • Scour depth increases with discharge intensity — discharge per metre of waterway. Halve the waterway and you have more than doubled the depth you must find.
    • It decreases with coarser bed material — the silt factor. Fine sand scours far deeper than shingle.
    • Local scour scales with pier width, and only weakly with everything else. Pier width is the dominant variable you control, and it is why a slender pier is worth real money.
    • It increases with skew. A pier at an angle to the flow presents a much greater effective width — a modest skew can be equivalent to a far wider pier.

    The debris raft. Trees and brushwood collect against a pier and form a raft, and that raft becomes part of the obstruction. Effective width can increase several times over, and local scour goes with it. On Nepali rivers in monsoon this is not an exceptional case — it is the normal condition of the flood peak. If your design considered a clean 1.5 m pier and the river presents it with a 6 m raft of timber, the scour you get bears no relation to the scour you calculated. Two conclusions follow: pier shape and spacing should discourage rafting, and the safest pier is the one that is not in the channel at all.

    Why low-water inspection misleads

    This deserves its own heading because it is the mechanism behind a great many surprises.

    Scour depth peaks at the flood peak, when velocity is highest. As the flood recedes, velocity falls, the river's transport capacity collapses, and the material it is carrying drops out — much of it straight back into the hole. What refills the hole is loose, uncompacted sediment with almost no bearing capacity, but it looks exactly like bed.

    So a dry-season inspection can show a normal bed over a pier that was standing on a fraction of its intended embedment three months earlier. Three practical consequences:

    • Do not take low-water bed level as evidence that scour is not occurring. It is evidence of nothing.
    • Use methods that see through the refill. Probing or sounding will often push easily through loose infill and meet resistance at the true scoured level. Repeat surveys at the same sections, year on year, are worth more than any single measurement.
    • Look at the apron, not the bed. Displaced, launched or missing apron material is the surviving evidence of what the flood did, and unlike the bed it does not tidy itself up.

    What to do about it

    1. Found below total scour, with grip. The primary defence, and the only one that does not require maintenance. Everything else is secondary.
    2. Do not put piers in the channel if a single span will reach. This is the highest-value decision available and it is made at concept stage or not at all.
    3. Give the river a generous waterway. Reduces contraction scour, reduces afflux, and reduces the chance of blockage.
    4. Shape and align the piers. Round or streamlined noses, aligned with the flow — and remember the flow direction at flood, which is not always the direction at low water.
    5. Aprons and riprap. A launching apron is designed to fall into the developing scour hole and armour it. It works, and it is a maintenance commitment: check and replenish after every significant flood, or it is a one-shot defence that was already spent years ago.
    Judgment

    Treat founding depth as the one thing you do not economise on. Every other scour measure — apron, riprap, guide bund — depends on somebody maintaining it for the next fifty years, and you have no control over that; founding depth depends on nobody. When you are asked to shorten a span to save money, price the foundation depth that the extra contraction scour will demand, and put both numbers in the same table. And on any existing bridge you are responsible for, get a bed profile surveyed at the same sections every year after monsoon. Three years of that is worth more than any calculation, because it is the only way to see a trend that a single low-water inspection is designed to hide.

    Field assignment

    Go to a bridge at low water — an older one, with piers in the channel. Then:

    1. Look at the apron around each pier. Is it intact, displaced, partly launched, or gone? Photograph it.
    2. Compare the bed level against a fixed reference on the pier — a construction joint, a stain line, the top of the footing if visible. Is more or less of the pier showing than you would expect?
    3. If you can safely reach it, probe the bed beside the pier with a rod. Does it push in easily, and how far before it meets resistance?

    What you probably found: a bed that looked entirely normal, and a rod that went in further beside the pier than away from it. That difference is the refilled scour hole — loose sediment sitting where compact bed should be. It is the cheapest scour investigation there is, and almost nobody does it.

    Check yourself

    1 · Name the three components of total scour and say which one your design decisions control most directly.

    General (long-term and flood-period lowering of the whole reach), contraction (caused by the bridge narrowing the waterway) and local (the hole around a pier or abutment). Contraction scour is the one you control most directly, through the waterway you provide.

    2 · Why can a dry-season inspection show a normal bed over a pier that was severely scoured months earlier?

    Because scour peaks at the flood peak and refills as the flood recedes and transport capacity collapses. The hole fills with loose, uncompacted sediment that looks identical to bed but has almost no bearing capacity. Low-water bed level is evidence of nothing.

    3 · What is a debris raft and why does it invalidate a local scour calculation?

    Trees and brushwood collecting against a pier, forming a much larger obstruction. Local scour scales with effective pier width, so a raft can multiply the width several times and produce scour bearing no relation to the calculated value. On Nepali rivers in monsoon it is the normal condition, not an exception.

    4 · A client asks you to shorten a bridge by removing one span. What must be in your reply?

    That narrowing the waterway raises discharge intensity and therefore contraction scour, so the foundations must go deeper — and both costs belong in the same table. A shorter deck is not automatically a cheaper bridge; some of the saving is simply moved underground.

    5 · Why is founding depth privileged above aprons, riprap and guide bunds?

    Because every other measure depends on somebody maintaining it for the structure's whole life, and you cannot control that. Founding depth requires nobody. It is the only scour defence that is not a maintenance commitment.

    Go deeper — regime scour, worked from discharge to founding level≈ 20 min · at a desk
    Lacey's regime theory, and why South Asia uses it

    Lacey's approach starts from an observation about alluvial rivers in the Indian subcontinent: a river carrying a given discharge through a given bed material will, left alone, settle into a characteristic width, depth and slope — its regime. Depart from regime and the river works to return to it, and scour is one of the ways it does that.

    Two relations do the work. The regime wetted perimeter:

    P = 4.75 √Q P in metres, Q the design discharge in m³/s. For a wide shallow channel P is close enough to the surface width.

    and the regime scour depth, which is what you actually design to:

    dsm = 1.34 (q² / f)1/3 dsm = mean regime scour depth below highest flood level, m · q = discharge intensity = Q / effective waterway, m³/s per m · f = Lacey's silt factor

    with the silt factor taken from the bed material:

    f = 1.76 √d50 d50 the mean particle diameter in millimetres. Units matter here and it is the commonest slip in the whole calculation.

    Read the exponents before you use them, because they contain the design lessons:

    • dsm ∝ q2/3. Narrow the waterway and scour rises — not linearly, but at the two-thirds power. This is the arithmetic behind session 17's warning about shortening a bridge.
    • dsm ∝ f−1/3. Coarse beds scour much less. A gravel-bed hill river and a sand-bed Terai river are genuinely different problems, and the same discharge gives very different depths.
    • Nothing about pier width appears. Lacey is a regime method; the effect of the pier enters only through a multiplying factor. That is a real limitation, and it is why the debris-raft case at the end has to be handled another way.
    The multiplying factors

    dsm is a mean. Maximum scour at any particular point is obtained by multiplying it, with the factor depending on where you are in the river and what you have put in it. IRC bridge practice sets these out roughly as follows:

    LocationFactor × dsm
    Straight reach1.27
    Moderate bend1.50
    Severe bend1.75
    Right-angled bend, or severe swirls2.00
    Nose of a pier2.00

The governing values are in the IRC bridge codes as adopted in Nepali practice, and they are revised. A design justified against a remembered factor is a design with a hole in it.

    Where Lacey stops: pier width and the debris raft

    The regime method handles the pier through a blanket factor of 2.0, which means it cannot tell you what happens if the pier gets effectively wider. For that you need a local-scour formulation that carries pier width explicitly. The HEC-18 form used in American practice is:

    ys = 2.0 · K1K2K3 · y10.35 · a0.65 · Fr10.43 ys local scour depth · a pier width · y1 approach depth · Fr1 approach Froude number · K factors for nose shape, flow angle and bed condition

    The term to hold on to is a0.65. Local scour goes with pier width to the power 0.65, so:

    Effective pier widthMultiplier on local scour

    Modest debris raft, 2a1.57
    Substantial raft, 3a2.04

    • Rafting is a reason to prefer fewer, wider-spaced piers — or none. Two piers that each collect a raft are worse than one that does.
    • Where the pier stays, the founding level should carry margin explicitly for rafting rather than relying on the regime factor of 2.0 to have quietly covered it. It has not.
    Checking the answer without the formulae

    Three sanity checks worth doing every time, because arithmetic slips in this calculation are common and consequential:

    1. Is dsm plausible against the river? Regime scour depths on Nepali rivers of this size land in the range of a few metres to about ten. A result of 0.6 m or 60 m means a units error — almost always d50 entered in metres instead of millimetres.
    2. Does the waterway ratio explain the depth? If you are near regime width, expect modest scour. If your computed depth is large but your waterway is generous, something is wrong.
    3. What do the neighbours say? Existing bridges on the same river are data. If yours is founded five metres deeper or shallower than a comparable structure a kilometre away, find out which of you is wrong before you build.

    18 · Training the river

    Session 18 · core · 15 min

    Walk any protected bank in Nepal after a monsoon and you will find the same thing: a wall of gabion boxes, structurally intact, sitting several metres out in the river with the bank behind it gone — or standing perfectly straight with its bottom row hanging in mid-air. The boxes did not fail. The wire held, the stone stayed in, the workmanship was fine. The river simply went underneath. Almost everything built to protect a bank in this country fails the same way, and it is not a materials problem.

    The idea

    Bank protection fails from the toe up and from the ends in. The structure you can see is rarely what decides the outcome — what decides it is what happens below the bed and beyond the last box.

    What you are actually trying to do

    Training works have three distinct purposes, and confusing them is where the money goes wrong:

    • Protect — armour a bank so the flow cannot erode it. Revetment, pitching, gabion mattress.
    • Deflect — push the attacking current away from a bank. Spurs and groynes.
    • Guide — make the river approach a structure where and how you want. Guide bunds at a bridge.

    Armouring a bank that is under direct attack from a migrating bend is expensive and temporary; deflecting the attack first, then armouring what remains, is usually cheaper and lasts. Ask which of the three you need before you price anything.

    The toe, which is the whole game

    A launching apron is a deliberately sacrificial layer of stone laid on the bed in front of the structure. It is designed to fall — that is its function, not a defect. The design questions are how much stone (enough to cover the launched slope down to anticipated scour depth, at an adequate thickness) and how far out to lay it.

    Three things that go wrong with aprons in practice, all of which you can catch on site:

    • It was never built. The most common. Aprons are below water for part of the year, easy to omit, and invisible in a completed-works photograph.
    • The stone is too small. Launched stone must resist the velocity in the scour hole, which is higher than the average channel velocity. Undersized stone launches and then washes away, which is worse than useless — it looks like protection and provides none.
    • It launched years ago and nobody replenished it. An apron is a consumable. After a significant flood it must be inspected and topped up. This almost never happens, and it is why so many structures are protected only in the documentation.

    Spurs: deflecting the attack

    A spur projects into the flow and pushes the main current away from the bank. Classified by what they do to the flow — attracting (angled downstream, drawing flow towards them), deflecting (roughly normal to the bank), and repelling (angled upstream, throwing the current away). Repelling spurs angled upstream are the common choice for bank protection.

    They can be impermeable — solid boulder, gabion or earth-cored — which divert flow abruptly and generate severe local scour at the nose, or permeable — timber, bamboo, or porcupine-type frames — which slow the flow and encourage deposition rather than blocking it. Permeable spurs are cheaper, more forgiving, well suited to heavily sediment-laden rivers, and much more common in Nepali practice than the international literature suggests.

    The recurring errors with spurs are geometric, not structural:

    • Too short to reach the attacking current, so the river simply flows between the spur nose and the thalweg and carries on eroding.
    • Spaced too far apart, so the flow re-attaches to the bank between them and scallops it out. Spacing is conventionally set as a multiple of spur length — check the current standard for the ratio and do not guess it.
    • No protection at the nose, where the worst local scour is guaranteed.
    • The first spur is at the wrong end. The upstream-most spur takes the hardest attack and needs to be the strongest — it is often the weakest, because it was built last as the works crept upstream.

    Guide bunds and outflanking

    At a bridge across a wide alluvial river, guide bunds confine the flow so it approaches the opening axially rather than at an angle, keeping the current off the approach embankments. Their essential feature is the curved head upstream, which turns the flow smoothly instead of presenting it with a blunt end to attack.

    The failure mode to fear is outflanking: the river goes around the end of your work rather than through it. It applies to every kind of training structure and it is the second great killer after toe scour. The rule is simple and constantly broken — tie the ends into ground the river cannot get behind. A protection work that stops at a property boundary, at the end of a contract length, or where the budget ran out is not protection. It is a well-built starting point for erosion, because you have concentrated the attack precisely at its end.

    Everything here transfers the problem. Deflecting flow away from one bank sends it somewhere else — usually to the opposite bank, or downstream to whoever is next. On a village-by-village basis this is how a river gets progressively squeezed by a series of individually sensible works, each making the next one necessary. If you are designing one reach, at least look at what your work does to the reach below, and say so. It will occasionally change the design; it will always change what you can honestly promise.

    Judgment

    Inspect training works the way the river attacks them: start at the toe and the ends. Is there an apron, is the stone big enough, and has it been replenished since it last launched? Are the ends keyed into ground the river cannot get behind? Those two questions will diagnose the great majority of failures, and neither requires a calculation. And when you specify protection, put the apron replenishment into the maintenance schedule with a named owner — an unreplenished apron is a one-shot defence that everybody believes is permanent.

    Field assignment

    Find a length of bank protection or a set of spurs on a river you know. At low water:

    1. Look at the toe. Can you see an apron? Is the stone large, or is it the same size as the fill? Has it launched — is it sloping down into a hollow?
    2. Walk to both ends. What are they tied into? Is there erosion starting just beyond the last box?
    3. If there are spurs, look at whether the flow re-attaches to the bank between them — scalloped bank between spur noses is the giveaway.

    What you probably found: erosion starting immediately beyond the downstream end of the works, and no visible apron. Those two account for most bank protection failure in Nepal, and both are decided before a single box is filled — one in the design, one in the schedule of quantities.

    Check yourself

    1 · A gabion revetment is found intact but lying tilted into the river, with the bank behind it gone. What failed?

    Not the revetment — the bed beneath it. Scour undermined the toe and the structure rotated into the hole. This is the standard failure mode, and it is why toe protection, not wall design, decides the outcome.

    2 · What is a launching apron meant to do, and why is it not a defect when it moves?

    It is sacrificial stone laid flat on the bed beyond the toe, designed to slide down into the developing scour hole and armour it. Launching is its function. The design questions are stone size — sized for scour-hole velocity, not average channel velocity — and having enough of it to cover the launched slope.

    3 · Name the two most common geometric errors with spurs.

    Making them too short to reach the attacking current, so the river flows between the nose and the thalweg and keeps eroding; and spacing them too far apart, so flow re-attaches between them and scallops the bank. Both are geometry errors, not structural ones.

    4 · What is outflanking, and what is the rule that prevents it?

    The river going around the end of the works rather than through them. The rule is to tie the ends into ground the river cannot get behind. Protection that stops at a boundary or where the budget ran out concentrates attack at its end — it is a starting point for erosion, not a defence.

    5 · Why are permeable spurs often preferred on heavily sediment-laden Nepali rivers?

    Because they slow the flow and encourage deposition rather than diverting it abruptly. That avoids the severe nose scour that impermeable spurs generate, costs less, and works with the river's sediment load instead of against it.

    19 · A design flood that is moving

    Session 19 · 13 min

    Every method in Act III rests on one assumption, and nobody says it out loud: that the future will resemble the past. Frequency analysis, regional formulae, the whole apparatus of return periods — all of it assumes the record you are extrapolating from was generated by a system that is still the same system. In Nepal it is not. The glaciers are retreating, the rainfall is intensifying, and the catchments are being rebuilt by roads. Your hundred-year flood is a moving target, and pretending otherwise is the most respectable error in the whole discipline.

    The idea

    Statistical hydrology assumes stationarity — that the distribution of floods is fixed. When the climate and the catchment are both changing, that assumption fails, and no amount of extra precision in the arithmetic repairs it. The honest response is not a better number. It is a design that tolerates being wrong.

    What "non-stationary" actually means

    Fit a Gumbel distribution to forty years of annual maxima and you get a hundred-year flood. The fit is only meaningful if those forty years are forty samples from one distribution. If the distribution is drifting, you have averaged a moving thing and produced a number that describes neither the past nor the future.

    Three separate drifts are running in Nepal at once, and it matters that they are independent — because they compound:

    What is changingEffect on your design flood
    Rainfall intensity. A warmer atmosphere holds more moisture, and extreme short-duration bursts intensify faster than seasonal totalsHits small steep catchments hardest — session 13's short time of concentration is exactly the duration that is intensifying. Cross-drainage is the most exposed asset class you own
    Glaciers and glacial lakes. Retreating ice is creating and enlarging moraine-dammed lakesThe GLOF source population is growing. A hazard whose frequency cannot be inferred from history, because the sources did not exist
    The catchment itself. Roads, deforestation, terrace abandonment, urbanisationRunoff coefficient up, time of concentration down, sediment yield up. This one has nothing to do with climate, and on many catchments it is the larger effect

    That third row deserves emphasis, because it is the one an engineer can be honest about without any climate argument at all. A catchment that has been roaded and cleared since the gauge record began is producing a different hydrograph from the same rainfall. The record was made by a catchment that no longer exists. You do not need a climate model to accept that; you need a satellite image from twenty years ago.

    What not to do

    Two responses are common and both are worse than the problem.

    Ignoring it — carrying on with stationary frequency analysis and treating the output as a fact. This is the default, it is defensible in an audit, and it quietly under-designs the assets most exposed to the change.

    Over-claiming — bolting a climate factor onto the discharge and presenting the result with the same confidence as before. Downscaled projections at the scale of a single Nepali hill catchment carry very wide uncertainty, and a single multiplier converts honest uncertainty into false precision. If you apply a factor, say what it is, where it came from, and how uncertain it is.

    The trap is treating uncertainty as a number problem. It is a design problem. If you cannot know the discharge to better than a factor of two, the right question stops being "what is Q" and becomes "which design is least sensitive to being wrong about Q". Those two questions have different answers, and only the second one can actually be delivered.

    Designing to tolerate being wrong

    Five strategies, roughly in order of value per rupee. None requires a climate projection.

    1. Test the sensitivity, and say what you found. Run the design at your Q, and at 1.5× and 2×. Report what breaks and when. This costs an afternoon, requires no new science, and turns an unquantifiable risk into a stated one. It is also the single most persuasive page you can put in front of a client.
    2. Buy margin where it is cheap. Freeboard, a larger pipe, one more span, a deeper foundation — these are inexpensive at construction and impossible later. Session 14's ranking applies: generosity in opening size and founding depth outperforms precision in Q.
    3. Design for graceful failure. Decide now what happens when it is exceeded. Approaches that wash out and are rebuilt in a fortnight, a deck that overtops without being lifted, an apron that launches. A structure with a designed failure mode is a structure that survives an unpredicted event as a repair rather than a replacement.
    4. Prefer what can be upgraded. A culvert that can be twinned, an embankment that can be raised, a wall founded deep enough to be extended. Optionality is worth real money when the design condition is drifting.
    5. Fund the maintenance. The most reliable response to a wetter, flashier future is a drain that is cleared before every monsoon. It is unglamorous, it is where session 10 ends up, and no amount of design compensates for its absence.

    Being honest on paper

    Two sentences on a drawing do more for the next engineer than a chapter of hedging:

    • State the basis. Q, return period, method, source, date — session 14's rule. Someone assessing this in twenty years needs to know what you assumed, not what you hoped.
    • State the sensitivity. "At 1.5×Q the deck overtops; the structure and foundations are unaffected and the approaches require reinstatement." That single line tells an owner exactly what they have bought, and it is the difference between a design that was exceeded and a design that failed.
    Judgment

    Stop trying to compute a future discharge you cannot know, and start designing so that being wrong about it is survivable. Run the sensitivity, put the margin where it is cheap, decide the failure mode deliberately, and write both the basis and the sensitivity on the drawing. And when you are challenged on the climate argument — you will be — fall back on the one that needs no projection at all: the catchment above this structure has been roaded and cleared since the gauge record was made, so the record was produced by a catchment that no longer exists. That is observable, local, and very hard to argue with.

    Field assignment

    Take a catchment you know that has a structure at its outlet, and compare it against itself:

    1. Find the oldest satellite imagery available and the newest.
    2. Estimate what changed — roads cut, forest lost or gained, terraces abandoned, settlement spread.
    3. Ask which direction that pushes C and tc from session 13, and therefore the peak.
    4. Then look at the structure and ask what it does at 1.5× its design flow.

    What you probably found: substantially more road and more bare ground than when the structure was designed, and a structure with no obvious tolerance for exceedance. The first is evidence you can show anybody; the second is the thing worth fixing.

    Check yourself

    1 · What does stationarity mean, and why does its failure break frequency analysis?

    Stationarity is the assumption that the flood distribution is fixed, so a record is many samples from one distribution. If the distribution is drifting, fitting a curve to the record averages a moving thing — the resulting return period describes neither the past nor the future, and more precision does not repair it.

    2 · Which assets are most exposed to intensifying short-duration rainfall, and why?

    Small cross-drainage on steep catchments. Their time of concentration is minutes to tens of minutes, and short-duration extremes are intensifying faster than seasonal totals — so the critical storm duration for those catchments is exactly the duration that is changing most.

    3 · Give the argument for non-stationarity that requires no climate projection at all.

    The catchment has physically changed since the gauge record was made — roads cut, forest cleared, terraces abandoned — which raises the runoff coefficient and shortens the time of concentration. The record was generated by a catchment that no longer exists. That is observable from twenty-year-old imagery.

    4 · Why is bolting a single climate multiplier onto Q a poor response?

    Because downscaled projections for an individual Nepali hill catchment carry very wide uncertainty, and a single factor presents that uncertainty as a precise number. If a factor is used, its value, source and uncertainty must be stated — otherwise it converts honest doubt into false confidence.

    5 · Restate the design question when Q cannot be known better than a factor of two.

    Not "what is Q" but "which design is least sensitive to being wrong about Q". That shifts effort to sensitivity testing, cheap margin, deliberate failure modes, upgradability and funded maintenance — all deliverable, unlike a precise future discharge.

    20 · Alignment is the decision

    Session 20 · core · 15 min

    Everything else in this act is remedial. Walls, drains, bio-engineering, protection works — every one of them is money spent managing a consequence of where the line was drawn. And the line is usually drawn in a week, early, by people under pressure to connect two points, often before a geologist has seen the ground. That week determines what the road costs for the next fifty years, and no amount of excellent detailing afterwards recovers a bad decision made in it.

    The idea

    Alignment is the largest risk-reduction lever available on any road, because it is the only one that acts on exposure — and it is free at the moment it is taken and unaffordable ever after.

    Why it dominates everything

    Return to session 2's four terms. Design, detailing and construction quality all reduce vulnerability. Maintenance and redundancy build capacity. Only alignment changes exposure — whether the asset is in the way of the hazard at all. And session 10 adds the sharper point: alignment also determines how much new hazard the road manufactures, through the cuts it requires and the water it concentrates.

    So the alignment decision is the only one that moves two of the four terms at once, and one of them in a way nothing else can. It has one further property that makes it uncomfortable: the cost curve is brutally asymmetric.

    StageCost of moving the line 200 m
    Corridor selection, on a mapEffectively nothing
    Preliminary surveyA few days of survey
    Detailed designRedesign cost, programme slip
    After constructionPractically impossible. You manage it forever instead

    What a good alignment avoids

    In rough order of how much trouble each one causes:

    • Old landslides and colluvium. Session 3's dormant Middle Hills ground. Crossing the toe of an old slide is the most reliable way to reactivate it, and the resulting problem is deep-seated, expensive and permanent. This is the single most important thing to avoid, and the hardest to see, because dormant slide ground is gentle and cultivated — often the most attractive line on the hillside.
    • Debris fans and the mouths of steep tributaries. Session 8. Cross above the fan apex, where the channel is confined and you know where the flow will be, or below the fan toe. A mid-fan crossing is a commitment to rebuilding.
    • Adverse dip in rock. Session 11. The same cut on the other side of the valley may be entirely sound. A geological reconnaissance before the line is fixed is worth more than any subsequent bolting budget.
    • The migration corridor of a river. Session 15. Approach embankments, not spans, are what these rivers actually take out.
    • Springs, seepage lines and wet ground. Water in the slope is the session 6 mechanism waiting for a cut.
    • Long, high cut sections. Cut volume drives both cost and instability, and it generates the spoil that causes session 10's damage below the road.

    Ridge, valley or mid-slope

    Ridge alignments are the most stable choice available in Nepal and are under-used. There is almost no catchment above you, so there is little water to concentrate and little to fall on you; cuts are shallow; the ground is generally drier. The costs are real — longer routes, steeper climbs, and villages are usually not on ridges — but on a whole-life basis a ridge line frequently wins, and the argument is rarely made because the comparison is never run.

    Valley floor alignments are seductive: gentle gradients, short, easy setting-out. They are also in the way of the river, the fans and everything that comes down the sides. They demand river training in perpetuity.

    Mid-slope is where most roads end up, because that is where the settlements are. It is a legitimate answer — the road exists to serve people — but it carries the largest cut volumes, the largest catchment above, and the spoil problem. If you must be mid-slope, that is the moment to insist on mass balance and proper drainage rather than to accept them as optional.

    Doing it properly, with the time you actually have

    The full process is corridor selection, then reconnaissance, then preliminary, then detailed. In practice you will often be handed a line and asked to make it work. Two things are worth fighting for even then:

    1. A geological and geomorphological walk-over before the line is frozen. Two or three days, one experienced pair of eyes, session 9's method applied along the corridor. It routinely finds an old slide or an adverse dip that moves the line a hundred metres and saves a decade of remediation. This is the highest return on any expenditure in road engineering, and it is the item most often cut.
    2. Compare at least two corridors on whole-life terms. Not construction cost — construction plus fifty years of maintenance and closure. A ridge line that costs 20 % more to build and needs no annual slide clearance is not the expensive option, and the only way to demonstrate that is to put both columns in the same table.

    The political line. Alignments in Nepal are frequently decided by which settlements must be served, and that is legitimate — a road that misses the village fails at its purpose. The engineering job is not to overrule it but to price it honestly: to say that serving this settlement by this line requires these works and this annual maintenance, and to offer the alternative with its own numbers. Decisions made with the cost visible are usually better than decisions made without it, and where they are not, at least the record shows what was known.

    Judgment

    Treat the alignment as the design, and everything after it as consequence management. When you join a project, the first question is whether the line is still movable — and if it is, spend your effort there rather than on the structures. Insist on the walk-over before it freezes; it is the cheapest thing in the budget and the only one that can remove a problem instead of managing it. And when the line is fixed and wrong, say so in writing, price what it will cost to hold, and make sure that number is in front of whoever decided.

    Field assignment

    Take a hill road you know that has recurring problems, and re-plan it on imagery:

    1. Mark every section that has failed or been rebuilt repeatedly.
    2. Sketch an alternative corridor — a ridge line, or the other side of the valley — between the same two points.
    3. List what the alternative avoids, and what it costs: extra length, extra climb, settlements missed.

    What you probably found: an alternative that avoids most of the recurring failures and misses two villages. That is the real trade — not stability against cost, but stability against access. Naming it that way is more useful than pretending the existing line was simply a mistake.

    Check yourself

    1 · Why is alignment the largest risk-reduction lever on a road?

    Because it is the only decision that changes exposure — whether the asset is in the hazard's way at all — and it simultaneously determines how much new hazard the road manufactures through its cuts and concentrated drainage. It moves two of the four risk terms, one of them uniquely.

    2 · What makes the alignment cost curve so unforgiving?

    Moving the line is effectively free at corridor selection, cheap at preliminary survey, expensive at detailed design and impossible after construction — at which point you manage the consequence forever instead. The decision is cheapest exactly when least is known about it.

    3 · What is the single most important ground condition to avoid, and why is it hard to see?

    Old landslide deposits and colluvium. Cutting through the toe reactivates a dormant mass, producing a deep-seated, expensive, permanent problem. It is hard to see because such ground is gentle, terraced and cultivated — often the most attractive line available.

    4 · Give the case for a ridge alignment and the case against.

    For: almost no catchment above, so little water to concentrate and little to fall on you; shallow cuts; drier ground. Against: longer, steeper, and villages are not usually on ridges. On whole-life cost the ridge often wins, but the comparison is rarely run.

    5 · The alignment has been fixed politically to serve a settlement. What is the engineer's job?

    Not to overrule it but to price it honestly — to state what works and what annual maintenance this line requires, and to present the alternative with its own numbers. Decisions made with the cost visible are better; where they are not, the record at least shows what was known.

    21 · Drainage is everything

    Session 21 · core · 15 min

    Ask any overseer with twenty years on a hill road where it fails and he will take you to the same three or four places, every time, without hesitation. Then ask him why, and the answer is always some version of the water comes here. Not the rock, not the design load, not the earthquake. More hill road in Nepal is destroyed by mismanaged water than by every other cause combined — and drainage is simultaneously the cheapest element to build, the first to be value-engineered out, and the one nobody is funded to clean.

    The idea

    A road is a machine for collecting water. Once you accept that, drainage stops being a detail attached to the road and becomes the primary design — with one governing rule: know where every drop is going, all the way to a natural channel.

    The system, and what each part is for

    ElementJobHow it fails
    Camber / crossfallGet water off the running surface before it soaks in or runs longitudinallyLost to rutting and patching. A flat or reverse-cambered road delivers its water to the worst place available
    Side drain (hill side)Catch runoff from the cut face and the surface, and carry it to an outletUnlined so it leaks into the slope; silted so it overtops; or built with no fall
    Catch drain (above the cut)Intercept hillside runoff before it reaches the cut face at allUsually omitted. The single most valuable drain on a hill road and the one least often built
    Turnout / scupperGet water out of the side drain frequently, in small quantitiesToo few, so flow accumulates; or discharging onto bare hillside
    Cross-drainageTake a natural channel under the roadSession 22
    Chute / cascadeTake water down a steep slope without it cutting a gullyUnlined, undersized, or stopping short of a stable outlet
    Subsurface drainLower the water table in the slope or behind a wallOmitted, or blinded by fines because no filter was provided

    The four rules

    1 · Intercept above the cut. A catch drain along the top of the cut face stops hillside runoff before it can reach and erode the face, and before it can infiltrate into the ground you have just destabilised. It is cheap, it is the most effective single element in the system, and on most hill roads in Nepal it simply is not there. If you can persuade a client to add one thing, add this.

    2 · Get rid of water often, and in small amounts. Flow in a side drain accumulates along its length, and both velocity and volume rise with it. Turnouts at frequent intervals keep every discharge small enough for the receiving ground to absorb. The failure pattern is unmistakable: a long stretch with no turnout, and a serious gully at the point where the drain finally discharges. Many small releases are always safer than one large one.

    3 · Line the drain, or it is an infiltration trench. An unlined drain on a cut slope leaks along its full length, straight into the slope it is meant to protect. This is session 6's pore-pressure mechanism, installed deliberately at the worst possible location. Lining also lets you accept a higher velocity without scouring, which matters on grades.

    4 · Every outlet must reach a natural channel. Walk to the end of every drain on the drawing and ask what receives the water. A chute discharging onto open hillside is a gully that will start at the outlet and work its way headward — up the slope, towards the road — until it takes the shoulder. That process takes a few seasons and is entirely predictable.

    The one that gets forgotten: water behind the wall

    Retaining and breast walls fail more often from water than from load. Groundwater collects behind a wall, and hydrostatic pressure on the back face can easily exceed the earth pressure the wall was designed for. The remedies are cheap and standard — weep holes at close spacing, a free-draining backfill, and a filter between the backfill and the retained soil so that fines do not migrate in and blind it.

    Gabions are permeable, which is often taken to mean they need no drainage provision. They still need the water behind them to be able to get to them. A gabion wall backfilled with silty spoil is not a permeable structure; it is a dam with a permeable face. Session 23 returns to this.

    Drainage is a system, and a system needs an owner. Every element above depends on being cleared before the monsoon — a silted drain is worse than no drain, because it overtops in a concentrated stream at whatever point it happens to fail. A drainage design without a named, funded annual clearance responsibility is a drawing of a system, not a system. When you hand over a hill road, hand over the schedule too, and say plainly that the design assumes it.

    Judgment

    Design the drainage first and fit the road to it, rather than the reverse. Walk the whole line and follow the water: where does it come from, where does it enter, where does it leave, and what receives it. If you cannot answer the last question at every outlet on the drawing, the design is not finished. And if the budget is being cut, defend the catch drain and the outlet chutes ahead of almost anything else — those two items prevent more damage per rupee than any structure on the project.

    Field assignment

    Walk 500 metres of hill road, slowly, with a notebook. Follow the water:

    1. Is there a catch drain above the cut? (Usually not — note it.)
    2. Is the side drain lined, and does it have fall?
    3. Count the turnouts. What is the spacing?
    4. Walk to every outlet and photograph what receives the water.
    5. Mark every failure below the road, and its distance from the nearest outlet.

    What you probably found: no catch drain, an unlined drain, too few turnouts, and failures clustered within fifty metres downhill of the outlets. That last correlation, drawn on one sketch, is the most persuasive document you can produce about a road — because it is their road, and it converts an argument about the weather into an argument about three specific outlets.

    Check yourself

    1 · Why is a catch drain above the cut described as the most valuable single element?

    Because it intercepts hillside runoff before it reaches the cut face at all — preventing both erosion of the face and infiltration into ground the cut has already destabilised. It is cheap, highly effective, and on most Nepali hill roads it is simply absent.

    2 · Why are many frequent turnouts safer than a few large discharges?

    Flow accumulates along a side drain, so volume and velocity rise with distance. Frequent turnouts keep each discharge small enough for the receiving ground to absorb, whereas a long unrelieved stretch delivers one concentrated release that gullies the slope at the outlet.

    3 · What is an unlined side drain on a cut slope, functionally?

    An infiltration trench. It leaks along its whole length into the slope it is meant to protect, raising pore pressure exactly where the cut has already reduced the factor of safety. Lining also permits higher velocity without scour, which matters on grades.

    4 · A chute discharges onto open hillside. Describe what happens next and in which direction.

    A gully starts at the outlet and erodes headward — upslope, towards the road — over a few seasons, until it undermines and takes the shoulder. It is entirely predictable, which is why every outlet must reach a natural channel.

    5 · A gabion wall is permeable, so why can it still fail from water?

    Because permeability of the face is useless if the water behind cannot reach it. Backfilled with silty spoil and no filter, the wall becomes a dam with a permeable face — hydrostatic pressure builds on the back and can exceed the earth pressure it was designed for.

    22 · Cross-drainage

    Session 22 · core · 15 min

    A culvert is sized for a discharge. Then a tree arrives. The tree lodges across the inlet, brushwood packs in behind it, and within minutes the opening you calculated so carefully is a quarter of what it was. Water backs up, overtops the road, finds the fill, and takes fifteen metres of embankment out. The design discharge was correct. The design case was wrong — because on a Nepali khola in monsoon, the governing condition is not the flow. It is what the flow is carrying.

    The idea

    Size cross-drainage for the flow and the debris, and design the road so that when it blocks anyway — and it will — the water goes somewhere survivable instead of through the embankment.

    Choosing the structure

    TypeUse it whenWatch out for
    Pipe culvertSmall, clean catchments; short crossingsBlocks easily. Round openings are the worst shape for debris. Avoid below any catchment with stored material
    Box / slab culvertThe workhorse on hill roads. Wider opening for the same headroomNeeds a proper inlet and outlet apron; the outlet is where the scour is
    Causeway (Irish crossing)Wide, flashy, boulder-carrying channels where a bridge is unaffordableImpassable during flow. Needs a scour-proof downstream edge and guide posts
    Vented fordBaseflow through vents, floods over the top. A good compromise on debris-prone channelsThe vents block; the design must assume they have

    The design instinct to develop is this: on a debris-prone khola, a structure the flow passes over often outlasts one the flow has to pass through. A causeway that is overtopped twice a monsoon and remains intact is a better asset than a culvert that is destroyed once every four years, even though the causeway looks like the inferior solution on a drawing.

    Sizing, and the two things that undo it

    Start from session 13: a discharge computed with an intensity at the catchment's own time of concentration. Then apply the two corrections that most designs skip.

    Blockage. There is no universal factor, and anyone who gives you one is guessing. What you can do is judge it from the catchment — session 8's question: how much loose material and timber is stored above me? On a forested, steep, debris-loaded khola, assume a substantial proportion of the opening will be unavailable at the peak and size accordingly. The practical rules that follow are worth more than a coefficient:

    • Prefer one large opening to several small ones. Three 900 mm pipes and one 2.7 m box pass similar flow; the box is far harder to block, and a single blocked pipe out of three loses a third of your capacity immediately.
    • Never go below a minimum size you can physically enter to clean. A 600 mm pipe on a hill khola cannot be maintained and will not be. Around 900 mm is a common practical floor; check the current standard, and then apply judgment above it.
    • Flare and protect the inlet so material is guided in rather than caught across the face. A headwall with wing walls both improves hydraulic performance and reduces snagging.

    Afflux. Any constriction raises the water level upstream of it. That raised level is what floods land above the road, and it is what overtops a road that "passed" the discharge calculation. Two consequences: the upstream flood level, not the pipe capacity, may govern your sizing where there is anything to protect above the road; and freeboard must be measured from the afflux level, not the normal flood level.

    The outlet is where the damage happens

    A culvert concentrates and accelerates flow. Whatever emerges is faster and more erosive than what arrived, and it is being delivered to a single point on a slope that was previously receiving a diffuse trickle. Session 21's rule applies with force here: every outlet must reach a natural channel or a properly designed energy dissipator.

    The minimum at any hill culvert outlet is an apron, a cut-off wall at its end, and either a lined chute to a stable channel or a stepped cascade to break the energy. The failure sequence without them is completely standard: scour hole at the outlet, hole works headward, undermines the outlet structure, culvert loses its end, embankment follows.

    Symptom at a culvertWhat it is telling you
    Silt or debris standing in the barrelInsufficient fall, or the outlet is drowned. It will block

    Sediment fan and ponding upstream of the inletCapacity or gradient inadequate; afflux is already occurring
    Damp patch or settlement in the fill over the barrelWater is tracking along the outside of the barrel — piping. Serious, and often missed

    Designing for the blockage you cannot prevent

    Assume it blocks. Then ask where the water goes, and make that route survivable:

    1. Give it a low point to overtop at — a deliberately lowered, armoured section of road, away from the fill and away from the culvert itself, so that overtopping happens where you chose rather than where the embankment is highest.
    2. Armour the downstream shoulder at that point so overtopping does not start a breach.
    3. Keep the fill shallow over cross-drainage where you can. The deeper the fill, the more there is to lose when water finds its way into it.

    This is session 19's graceful failure, applied to the most numerous structure you own. A road that overtops at a chosen point and is scraped clean the next morning has performed well. A road that loses an embankment has not — and the difference between them is a design decision, not luck.

    Cross-drainage is where maintenance pays best and is funded worst. Clearing culvert inlets before the monsoon is the highest-return maintenance activity on any hill road, by a wide margin, and it requires no equipment. Where you have influence, tie it to somebody's name and somebody's budget line — and put the inlet where a person can actually reach it, because an inlet that requires descending a bank in flood conditions will not be cleared.

    Judgment

    Size for the debris, not just the discharge — one big opening rather than several small, nothing you cannot physically enter, a flared and protected inlet. Then design the failure: choose where the road overtops, armour it, and keep the fill shallow. And walk to every outlet before you sign, because that is where the structure will actually be lost. A culvert is not a pipe under a road; it is a small structure with an upstream flood level, a downstream erosion problem and a blockage regime, and it deserves five minutes of thought rather than a number from a standard drawing.

    Field assignment

    Inspect five culverts on one road, and fill in the same five lines for each:

    1. Size and type — and could you get inside it to clean it?
    2. Inlet — clear, partly blocked, or fully blocked? Is there a headwall?
    3. Outlet — is there an apron, and is there a scour hole?
    4. Upstream — any sign of ponding or a sediment fan?
    5. If it blocked completely, where would the water go?

    What you probably found: at least one blocked or half-blocked inlet out of five, no apron at most outlets, and no answer at all to the fifth question. That fifth question is the one worth introducing into your practice — it takes seconds and it changes what you specify.

    Check yourself

    1 · Why is one large opening preferable to several small ones of equivalent capacity?

    Because it is far harder to block. With three pipes, a single blockage removes a third of the capacity immediately; a single box of equivalent area passes the same flow and resists debris much better. Round openings are also the worst shape for debris.

    2 · What is afflux and why does it matter for freeboard?

    The rise in upstream water level caused by the constriction. It is what floods land above the road and what overtops a road that satisfied the discharge calculation. Freeboard must therefore be measured from the afflux level, not the normal flood level.

    3 · Describe the standard failure sequence at an unprotected culvert outlet.

    Flow emerges concentrated and accelerated, scours a hole at the outlet, the hole erodes headward and undermines the outlet structure, the culvert loses its end, and the embankment follows. An apron with a cut-off wall and a chute or cascade to a stable channel prevents it.

    4 · A damp patch and settlement appear in the fill above a culvert barrel. What is happening?

    Piping — water tracking along the outside of the barrel rather than through it, carrying fines with it. It is serious, frequently missed, and it progressively hollows the embankment from within.

    5 · You accept that a culvert will block. What three things make that survivable?

    Give the water a chosen low point to overtop at, away from the deepest fill; armour the downstream shoulder there so overtopping does not start a breach; and keep the fill shallow over cross-drainage. Overtopping at a chosen point is a morning's cleaning; a breach is a reconstruction.

    23 · Walls that hold

    Session 23 · core · 15 min

    The default answer to any instability in Nepal is a gabion wall. It is quick to procure, easy to build with local labour and stone, and it looks like action. It is also the wrong structure a great deal of the time — sometimes because a different wall was needed, often because no wall was needed and the money should have gone into drainage or regrading. A wall is the most expensive way to solve a problem that a drain would have solved, and the second most expensive way to fail to solve a problem that was never about retaining anything.

    The idea

    Before choosing a wall type, establish that a wall is the answer — and then remember that walls in Nepal fail overwhelmingly from water behind them and ground beneath them, not from the strength of the wall itself.

    First: is a wall the answer at all?

    Three questions, in order:

    1. What is actually moving, and how deep is it? Session 7. A wall founded above the slip surface of a deep-seated slide is a passenger — it will be carried along with everything else. If the failure is 15 m deep, a 4 m wall is decoration.
    2. Is this a water problem? Session 6. If the mass is moving because pore pressure is high, drainage may restore stability at a fraction of the cost — and without drainage, the wall will be resisting a force that keeps growing.
    3. Can geometry solve it? Unloading the head, flattening or benching the cut, loading the toe with the material you removed. Often cheaper than a structure, and it changes both sides of the stability ratio at once.

    Only when those are answered does the wall type question become meaningful.

    The choices

    TypeSuitsLimits
    Dry stoneLow heights, good stone, skilled local masons, remote sites. Fully permeable, flexible, repairable by the communityHeight-limited. Depends entirely on workmanship — a good dry stone wall is a skilled artefact, not a heap
    GabionThe Nepali default for good reasons: local stone, local labour, no formwork, tolerates settlement, permeable, repairableWire corrodes and abrades; needs correct mesh and coating. Boxes must be properly laced and packed or they slump. Not for high loads or where stone is poor
    Masonry / RCC gravity or cantileverHigher loads, urban sections, where settlement is not expected and quality control is availableRigid — it cracks rather than deforms if the foundation moves. Needs a competent founding stratum and real drainage provision
    Reinforced soilHigher walls, good tolerance of settlement, can use site-won fillRequires specification discipline and inspection during construction. Rarely maintainable by the community afterwards
    Breast wall (against a cut face)Retaining the face of a cut, resisting ravelling and shallow movementNot a landslide solution — it holds a face, not a mass
    Toe wallSupporting the base of a slope or fill, resisting underminingMust be founded below scour and below the failure surface, or it is pointless

    Notice the pattern in the "suits" column. In hill Nepal, the flexible, permeable, locally-repairable options — dry stone and gabion — win far more often than their engineering sophistication suggests, and for a reason that is not technical: they can be repaired by the people who live there, with materials that are already on site. A cracked RCC wall in a remote ward will still be cracked in ten years. A slumped gabion can be re-laced by a local mason next spring.

    What actually decides whether it stands

    Drainage behind it. Water accumulating behind a wall applies hydrostatic pressure that can exceed the earth pressure the wall was designed for, and it is by far the commonest cause of failure. You need three things together, and two out of three does not work:

    • Weep holes at close spacing, through the wall, at more than one level.
    • Free-draining backfill immediately behind the wall.
    • A filter — graded granular or geotextile — between that backfill and the retained soil. Without it, fines migrate in and blind the drainage within a season or two, and you have a wall with decorative weep holes.

    Foundation depth. A wall must be founded below the surface the ground intends to move on, and below scour if there is water at the toe. Session 17's principle applies here too: founding depth is the one defence that does not depend on anyone maintaining it.

    Toe protection. Where the toe is in or near a channel, the wall needs the same treatment as any river structure — session 18's apron. A wall undermined at the toe rotates outward and goes, exactly as the gabion revetment did.

    The construction details that decide gabions

    Gabions are simple, which is why they are built badly. Five things separate a wall that lasts twenty years from one that slumps in three:

    1. Lacing. Boxes must be laced to each other and to those above and below, along every edge. Unlaced boxes are a stack, not a structure.
    2. Packing. Stone placed by hand, tight, with the face stones set deliberately. Tipped stone leaves voids that settle and slump the face.
    3. Stone quality and size. Sound, durable rock, graded so it does not escape the mesh. Weathered stone from the cut is convenient and wrong.
    4. Mesh specification. Correct wire gauge and coating for the exposure. In an abrasive, sediment-laden channel, wire life is the wall's life.
    5. Foundation and step-back. Founded properly, stepped back into the slope, with each course seated on the one below — not battered by eye on the day.

    A wall does not stabilise a slope; it retains a face. This distinction is worth holding hard, because the two get conflated constantly. If a mass is moving on a deep surface, the honest answers are drainage, regrading, or moving the road — and a wall built instead of those will fail, having consumed the budget that could have paid for them. Being the person who says "this does not need a wall" is unpopular and is frequently the most valuable contribution available.

    Judgment

    Ask what is moving and how deep before you ask what the wall is made of. Then spend your attention on the three invisible things — drainage behind, foundation below the failure surface, protection at the toe — because that is where walls are actually won and lost, and they are the three items most likely to be trimmed when the price comes in. On site, inspect gabion lacing and hand-packing before the boxes are closed; after that you cannot see the defect until it slumps. And prefer the structure the community can repair, unless there is a clear reason not to.

    Field assignment

    Find three walls on a road you know — ideally one sound, one leaning, one failed. For each:

    1. Are there weep holes? Are they flowing, damp, or dry and blocked?
    2. Is the wall leaning, bulging, or cracked — and is the movement uniform or worse at one end?
    3. What is happening at the toe — undermined, scoured, buried?
    4. Is there any sign the ground above the wall is still moving — crown cracks, a bulge over the top?

    What you probably found: the failed one had no visible weep holes or dry blocked ones, and the ground above it was still moving. That combination — a wall asked to hold a slide, with nowhere for the water to go — is the standard Nepali wall failure, and both halves of it were decided before a stone was laid.

    Check yourself

    1 · Give the three questions to answer before choosing a wall type.

    What is moving and how deep is it — a wall founded above a deep slip surface is a passenger. Is this a water problem, in which case drainage may restore stability far more cheaply. And can geometry solve it — unloading the head, benching, loading the toe, which moves both sides of the stability ratio.

    2 · Name the three drainage elements a retaining wall needs, and what happens if the third is omitted.

    Weep holes at close spacing and more than one level, free-draining backfill immediately behind, and a filter between that backfill and the retained soil. Without the filter, fines migrate in and blind the drainage within a season or two — leaving a wall with decorative weep holes and full hydrostatic pressure behind it.

    3 · Why do flexible, permeable walls often outperform technically superior rigid ones in remote Nepal?

    Because they tolerate settlement and, decisively, they can be repaired by local people with materials already on site. A cracked RCC wall in a remote ward stays cracked for a decade; a slumped gabion can be re-laced by a local mason next season.

    4 · Distinguish a breast wall from a landslide remedy.

    A breast wall retains the face of a cut and resists ravelling and shallow movement. It does not stabilise a mass moving on a deep surface. Conflating the two produces walls that consume the budget and then fail, having never intersected the mechanism.

    5 · Which gabion construction defects can you no longer see once the boxes are closed?

    Lacing and hand-packing. Boxes must be laced along every edge to their neighbours above, below and beside, and stone must be placed by hand and tight. Tipped stone leaves voids that settle and slump the face — and neither defect is visible in a finished wall until it moves.

    24 · Bio-engineering

    Session 24 · core · 15 min

    Two cut faces on the same road, cut the same year. One is a bare scar shedding material into the drain every monsoon. The other is covered in amriso planted in lines across the slope, has not moved in a decade, and is being harvested for broom-making by the household below it. The second cost less. Nepal did not import this technique — it developed it, wrote the manuals that the rest of the world cites, and then, in the rush of the last two decades of road building, largely stopped doing it.

    The idea

    Vegetation is a structural material with quantifiable functions and honest limits. It works on shallow instability and surface erosion, it gets stronger with time rather than weaker, and it costs a fraction of masonry. It does nothing whatever for a deep-seated slide, and pretending otherwise discredits the whole method.

    What plants actually do, mechanically

    Treat this as engineering rather than landscaping, and there are five distinct functions. The first two are the ones that matter for stability:

    FunctionMechanismDepth it reaches
    ReinforceRoots add tensile strength to the soil, exactly as fibres do — they carry tension across a potential shear planeRoot depth. For grasses and shrubs, typically the top 0.5–1.5 m
    AnchorDeeper woody roots tie a surface layer to firmer material beneathA few metres at best, with the right species
    ArmourFoliage and litter absorb raindrop impact; stems slow surface flow, so it cannot detach particlesSurface
    DrainTranspiration lowers soil moisture and pore pressure through the growing seasonRoot zone — and it stops in the monsoon, when you need it most
    Support / catchStems and clumps physically hold small debris and stop material travellingSurface

    Now the limitation, stated as sharply as possible, because everything depends on it:

    The techniques, and what each is for

    Nepal's roadside bio-engineering practice — codified in the Department of Roads manuals developed through the 1990s and still the standard reference — sorts techniques by the direction the planting runs, because that is what determines the function:

    TechniqueWhat it doesUse where
    Contour grass linesSlows surface flow and traps moving material; reinforces the top layerGeneral surface protection on cut and fill faces
    Downslope grass linesLets water run cleanly between lines without concentrating it; good on erodible faces where you want drainage, not pondingSandy or highly erodible material
    Diagonal linesCompromise — some flow retardation, some drainageWhere neither pure contour nor downslope suits
    Brush layeringLive woody cuttings laid in trenches across the slope. They root, reinforce and act as small barriers. The strongest of the vegetative techniquesFill slopes, shallow slips
    PalisadesRows of live cuttings driven in across the slope, forming a small barrierTrapping debris on moderate slopes
    Live check damsCuttings across a small gully to trap sediment and reduce gradientSmall, actively eroding gullies
    FascinesBundles of live material in shallow trenches along the contourSurface stabilisation on wet slopes
    Jute nettingHolds the surface and seed until vegetation establishes, then rots awaySteep faces where planting alone would wash off
    Vegetated stone pitchingCombines structure and planting — stone for immediate protection, roots for the long termThe hybrid case below

    The species used are working plants, not ornamentals, and most of them have a local economic value that matters for the maintenance argument: amriso (broom grass, Thysanolaena maxima), babiyo (Eulaliopsis binata), khar, napier grass, various bamboos, and shrubs such as simali (Vitex negundo) which roots readily from cuttings.

    Time, and why it changes the comparison

    The one genuine weakness of vegetation is that it is weakest when you need it first. A newly planted slope offers almost nothing in its first monsoon, reaches useful strength over two or three seasons, and continues improving. A masonry wall is at full strength on the day it is finished, and declines from then on.

    Two things follow. First, planting must be timed — established before the monsoon that will test it, with the pre-monsoon planting season being the whole game. Second, and more importantly, the comparison between vegetation and structure should be made over the design life, not on day one. Structure that decays against vegetation that strengthens crosses over surprisingly early.

    The honest answer is usually both

    Bio-engineering is not an alternative to civil works and was never presented as one in the Nepali manuals. The intended practice is combination: a toe wall or check dam to hold the base and control the immediate mechanism, with vegetation above it doing the surface protection and shallow reinforcement that a wall cannot and should not be paying for.

    The typical hill cut in Nepal is best served by a modest structural element at the toe, proper drainage from session 21, and a planted face — and it is usually cheaper than the oversized wall that gets built instead.

    Two failure modes of bio-engineering, and neither is botanical. The first is grazing — goats will remove a new planting scheme in weeks, so protection or a community agreement is part of the design, not an afterthought. The second is misapplication: planting a face whose problem is a 12 m deep-seated slide, then recording the technique as ineffective when it fails. That misdiagnosis has done more damage to the standing of bio-engineering in Nepal than any technical shortcoming.

    Judgment

    Ask one question first: is the failure surface inside the root zone? If it is, vegetation is a real structural answer and probably the cheapest one. If it is not, say so plainly and do not let planting be used as cover for an unaddressed deep problem. Then design the combination rather than choosing a side — structure at the toe, drainage throughout, vegetation on the face — and plant before the monsoon, not after it. And when you can, choose species people want: a slope planted with amriso gets protected by the household harvesting it, which is a maintenance arrangement no contract can buy.

    Field assignment

    Find a cut face on a road you know that has vegetation established on it, and one that is bare. Compare them:

    1. What is growing on the vegetated one, and is it in lines — contour, downslope, diagonal — or self-seeded?
    2. Is either face shedding material into the drain?
    3. On the bare face, why did nothing establish — material, aspect, grazing, or simply that nobody planted it?
    4. Would the bare face's problem be solved by planting, or is something deeper moving?

    What you probably found: the vegetated face was self-seeded rather than planted, and the bare one is bare because it was cut too steep for anything to hold on — which is session 10, not a planting problem. Cut angle and planting are the same decision, taken at different times by different people.

    Check yourself

    1 · Name the five mechanical functions of vegetation on a slope and say which two matter for stability.

    Reinforce, anchor, armour, drain and catch. Reinforcement — roots carrying tension across a potential shear plane, like fibres — and anchoring into firmer material beneath are the two that affect stability. The others control surface erosion and moisture.

    2 · State the limit of bio-engineering in one sentence.

    It works only where the failure surface lies within the root zone, roughly the top 0.5–1.5 m for grasses and shrubs; below that no planting scheme reaches the mechanism at all.

    3 · Why does timing dominate the first year of a scheme?

    Because vegetation is weakest exactly when it is newest — it offers almost nothing in its first monsoon and reaches useful strength over two or three seasons. Planting must be established before the monsoon that will test it, which makes the pre-monsoon season decisive.

    4 · Why does the vegetation-versus-structure comparison change over the design life?

    Because a wall is at full strength the day it is finished and declines thereafter, while vegetation starts near zero and strengthens. Compared over the whole design life rather than on day one, the crossover comes surprisingly early.

    5 · What are the two non-botanical failure modes, and which has done more reputational damage?

    Grazing — goats can strip a new scheme in weeks, so protection or a community agreement is part of the design — and misapplication, planting a face whose real problem is a deep-seated slide. The second has done far more damage, because the technique gets recorded as ineffective when it was simply the wrong diagnosis.

    25 · Green Roads

    Session 25 · core · 14 min

    In the 1990s Nepali and expatriate engineers working on hill roads in the Palpa and Dhading districts noticed something that should have been obvious and was not: the roads built slowly, by hand, without a dozer, were outlasting the ones built fast with machinery. Not because hand labour is inherently better, but because building slowly forced a set of practices — balanced earthwork, no tipping, drainage first, planting as you go — that machine construction skips. They wrote it down. The result is one of Nepal's genuine contributions to civil engineering, and it is cited internationally while being steadily abandoned at home.

    The idea

    The Green Road approach is not a cheaper standard for poor districts. It is a construction method whose core insight is that most hill road damage is created during construction — so the way you build determines the road's whole life more than the way you design it.

    The principles

    Strip away the terminology and it comes down to six things:

    1. Mass balance. Cut volume is matched to fill volume along the alignment, so there is nothing to dispose of. This single principle removes session 10's largest damage mechanism — spoil tipped over the edge. It is the heart of the method.
    2. No side-tipping, ever. Where surplus is unavoidable it goes to designated, contained, drained disposal sites, not over the shoulder.
    3. Build the drainage first. Drainage is constructed as the alignment opens, not after the formation is complete. A road that goes through a monsoon without drainage has already been damaged.
    4. Slow, staged construction across seasons. Cut in one dry season, let the slope settle and reveal its weaknesses through a monsoon, then stabilise and finish. Machine construction compresses this into weeks and the slope's response arrives after handover.
    5. Bio-engineering from the start — session 24, planted as construction proceeds so it has established by the time it is needed.
    6. Labour-based, locally employed. Which is both a social objective and a technical one: hand work permits the precision that mass balance and staged cutting require, and it puts the people who will live with the road inside its construction.

    Why it produces a better road, not just a cheaper one

    The counter-intuitive claim is that a slower, hand-built road is technically superior on this terrain, and the reasons are specific:

    • No manufactured hazard below. Mass balance removes tipped spoil, which session 10 identified as the mechanism doing the most damage and getting the least attention.
    • The slope gets to answer. Cutting across seasons means the face reveals its weaknesses while the contractor is still present and while stabilising is cheap. Fast construction hands over a face that has never seen rain.
    • Precision on narrow benches. A dozer working on a steep hillside cuts what it can reach, which is often more than the design requires. Hand cutting can hold a designed line and angle.
    • Vegetation has a head start. Planted during construction rather than after it, and therefore established when the first monsoon arrives.

    The honest limits

    It is not the answer everywhere, and being straight about that protects the method's credibility:

    LimitWhat it means
    TimeSeveral seasons rather than one. Politically difficult where access is promised before an election — which is session 4's problem, not an engineering one
    Traffic classSuited to low-volume local roads. It is not a method for a strategic highway carrying heavy vehicles
    RockHard rock sections need machinery or blasting; hand methods have a real limit
    SupervisionLabour-based work needs more technical supervision, not less. Without it, "labour-based" degrades into unsupervised digging
    Institutional memoryThe manuals exist; the trained supervisors and the funding model have thinned. This is the real constraint today

    What happened, and what is worth salvaging

    After federalisation gave local governments both the authority and the budget to build roads (session 4), the dominant model became the hired excavator: fast, visible, and cheap per kilometre on the day. Green Road practice requires patience, supervision and a multi-season programme, and it lost the comparison at the point where the comparison is made — construction cost.

    The argument that wins, when it wins, is the whole-life one from session 20: a track that is re-cut every year after every monsoon has not been built once cheaply, it has been built repeatedly and expensively, while degrading the hillside a step further each time. That case is winnable, and it needs the numbers put side by side.

    Even where the full method is impossible, the principles unbundle. If you can get only one thing into a specification, take mass balance and the prohibition on side-tipping — that alone removes the largest damage mechanism, and it can be written into any contract regardless of whether the rest of the approach is adopted.

    The alternative is rarely "no road". When you argue against fast dozer construction, you will be told that the community needs access and cannot wait. The honest counter is not that they should go without — it is that a badly cut track delivers access that is lost for part of every monsoon, degrades the land the community farms, and consumes its own capital cost again every few years. The choice is not road against no road. It is a road that lasts against a road that has to be rebuilt.

    Judgment

    Carry the principles even where you cannot carry the programme. In any hill road specification you influence, insist on two clauses: earthwork shall be balanced along the alignment and no side-tipping of spoil, with designated disposal sites where surplus is unavoidable. Add drainage constructed ahead of formation if you can get a third. Those three items are most of the benefit, they cost almost nothing to write, and they survive being handed to a contractor with an excavator — which is who will actually build the road.

    Field assignment

    Find two hill roads near you built by different methods — an older, hand-built or staged road, and a recent dozer-cut track. Walk or drive a kilometre of each:

    1. Look below the road. Is there tipped spoil? How far down does the disturbance reach?
    2. Look at the cut face. Is it a designed angle with benches, or whatever the machine achieved?
    3. Is there drainage, and is there vegetation established on the faces?
    4. Ask anyone local how often each road is closed or re-cut.

    What you probably found: the older road's damage stops at its own formation, while the new one has disturbed a wide band of hillside below it — and the closure answer confirmed it. That band of disturbance below the road is the clearest visual evidence there is for the whole argument, and it photographs well.

    Check yourself

    1 · What is mass balance, and why is it the heart of the method?

    Matching cut volume to fill volume along the alignment so there is no surplus to dispose of. It is central because it eliminates side-tipped spoil — the mechanism that loads the slope below, buries natural drainage and stockpiles debris-flow material. The slope below the road is left as it was.

    2 · Why does building across several seasons produce a technically better road?

    Because the cut face passes through a monsoon while the contractor is still present, revealing its weaknesses when stabilising is still cheap. Fast construction hands over a face that has never seen rain, so the slope's response arrives after handover and becomes somebody else's maintenance problem.

    3 · Give three honest limits of the Green Road approach.

    It takes several seasons rather than one, which is politically hard where access is promised quickly; it suits low-volume local roads, not strategic highways; and hard rock still needs machinery. It also needs more technical supervision, not less — without it, labour-based degrades into unsupervised digging.

    4 · If you can get only one or two clauses into a specification, which do you take?

    Earthwork balanced along the alignment, and no side-tipping of spoil with designated contained disposal sites where surplus is unavoidable. Those two capture most of the benefit, cost nothing to write, and survive being handed to a contractor with an excavator.

    5 · How do you answer "the community needs access now and cannot wait"?

    That the choice is not road against no road, but a road that lasts against a road that must be rebuilt. A badly cut track gives access that is lost for part of every monsoon, degrades the land the community farms, and consumes its own capital cost again every few years.

    26 · Bridges under hazard

    Session 26 · core · 15 min

    Go and look at the bridges lost in Nepal over the last thirty years and a pattern emerges that most engineers do not expect. The deck is usually intact. The girders are usually fine. What has gone is the ground — a pier undermined, an abutment slipped, an approach embankment washed out, leaving a perfectly sound superstructure spanning a gap it can no longer reach. Bridge design attention concentrates above the bearing line. Bridge losses concentrate below it.

    The idea

    A bridge is lost at its interfaces — foundation to river, abutment to slope, approach to bridge. Seismic design matters and is not usually what takes the structure. Scour and the approaches are.

    The approach embankment: the most-lost element

    It is worth saying this first because it is where the money is least often spent. The embankment is not part of the bridge in most people's minds, but functionally it is: if it goes, the bridge is unusable, and it goes far more often than any other element.

    Three mechanisms:

    • Outflanking. Session 18. The river goes round the end of the works and attacks the embankment from behind. The classic loss on a braided or migrating river, where the structure is fine and the road either side of it has gone.
    • Overtopping and breach. Flood level exceeds the embankment, water flows across it, and the downstream shoulder erodes into a breach. Preventable by armouring the shoulder at a chosen overtopping point — session 22's principle at a larger scale.
    • Settlement at the interface. A step between a rigid abutment on foundations and a compressible embankment. It is a serviceability problem in normal times and a serious one after an earthquake, when differential settlement can be large enough to stop vehicles entirely — which matters because those are exactly the vehicles bringing help.

    Foundations and the water

    Session 17 in one line: founding depth below total scour, plus grip. It is the only scour defence that does not depend on somebody maintaining it, and it is the decision that most reliably separates bridges that survive from bridges that do not.

    Two additions specific to bridge work:

    Keep piers out of the channel where a span can reach. A pier is an obstruction, an obstruction collects debris, and a debris raft multiplies local scour — session 17's a0.65. A single span with abutments on the banks removes the mechanism entirely, and on a Nepali khola it is frequently worth the extra structure cost against the whole-life alternative.

    Assess existing foundations honestly. On an older bridge you often do not know the founding level. The reflex is to assume it is adequate because the bridge is standing. It is standing because the design flood has not yet coincided with the bed's lowest state. Where records are absent, probing, sounding and repeat bed surveys are the available evidence — and repeat surveys at the same sections are worth more than any single investigation.

    Where seismic design actually bites

    Seismic design of a bridge is not a scaled-down building problem, and three things carry most of the risk:

    1 · Unseating. The dominant collapse mechanism for simply supported spans — and most Nepali bridges are simply supported. Relative displacement between deck and substructure exceeds the seating length and the span falls off its bearing. The remedies are unglamorous and highly effective: generous seat width, and restrainers or stoppers that limit displacement. Seat width is nearly free at design stage and cannot be added later.

    2 · Liquefaction and lateral spreading. Saturated loose sand near a river — which is exactly where abutments sit — can lose strength during shaking. Beyond bearing loss, the ground can spread laterally towards the channel, dragging abutments and piled foundations with it. This deserves a specific check anywhere you have young saturated alluvium, and the Terai crossings are the obvious candidates.

    3 · Abutment and backfill behaviour. Backfill pressure rises during shaking, and an abutment can be pushed, rotated or slid. Combined with settlement of the approach fill, this is what produces the step that stops traffic.

    Note what is not at the top of that list: the strength of the girders. Members sized for traffic loading are rarely what fails.

    Detailing that matters disproportionately

    • Bearings and expansion joints — the most neglected maintenance item on Nepali bridges. Seized bearings transfer forces the substructure was never designed for, and blocked joints do the same. They are cheap to inspect and are almost never inspected.
    • Deck drainage — water discharged at the abutment saturates the backfill and washes out the approach. Take it clear, to a channel.
    • Approach slabs — bridge the settlement step between abutment and embankment, and are worth their cost.
    • Access for inspection — if you cannot reach the bearings and the bearing shelf, they will not be inspected for the life of the structure.

    The post-earthquake question is different from the design question. Design asks whether it collapses. Response asks whether it can carry a loaded truck the following morning — session 29. A bridge that survives structurally but has a 400 mm step at the abutment is, for the first critical week, closed. Seat width, restrainers, approach slabs and compacted well-drained approach fill are life-safety measures at the network level, not merely serviceability items.

    Judgment

    Spend your attention below the bearing line. Get founding depth right, keep piers out of the channel if a span can reach, and treat the approach embankment as part of the bridge — tied into ground the river cannot get behind, armoured where it will overtop, and drained. On the seismic side, buy seat width and restrainers, and check for liquefiable ground at the abutments before anything else. And on any existing bridge you are responsible for: survey the bed at the same sections every year after monsoon, and open the bearings. Those two habits will find more real risk than any recalculation of the superstructure.

    Field assignment

    Inspect one bridge, from the ground rather than the deck:

    1. Walk to both approach embankments. What are the ends tied into? Any erosion starting behind the abutment?
    2. Look at the bearing shelf — can you even see it? How much seat is there beyond the bearing?
    3. Look at the bed around any pier, and at the apron. Probe if you safely can.
    4. Where does deck drainage discharge — clear of the structure, or onto the abutment backfill?

    What you probably found: deck drainage discharging straight down onto the abutment, and no access to inspect the bearings. Both are cheap to fix, both are ignored for decades, and the first is a slow reliable way to lose an approach.

    Check yourself

    1 · Where are bridges actually lost, and where does design attention usually go?

    Lost at the interfaces below the bearing line — foundation to river, abutment to slope, approach to bridge. Design attention concentrates on the superstructure, which is rarely what fails; the deck and girders are typically intact when a bridge is lost.

    2 · Name the three ways an approach embankment is lost.

    Outflanking, where the river goes round the end of the works and attacks from behind; overtopping and breach of the downstream shoulder; and settlement at the interface with the rigid abutment, producing a step that can stop vehicles entirely.

    3 · What is unseating, and what are the two cheap defences?

    Relative displacement between deck and substructure exceeding the seating length, so a simply supported span falls off its bearing — the dominant seismic collapse mechanism for the span types common in Nepal. The defences are generous seat width and restrainers or stoppers. Seat width is nearly free at design and cannot be added later.

    4 · Why is liquefaction a specific concern at bridge abutments?

    Because abutments sit in young saturated alluvium beside a river — exactly the material that loses strength under shaking. Beyond bearing loss, the ground can spread laterally towards the channel, dragging abutments and even piled foundations with it.

    5 · Why is a 400 mm step at an abutment a life-safety issue rather than a serviceability one?

    Because the post-earthquake question is not whether the bridge stood but whether it can carry a loaded truck the next morning. A structurally intact bridge with a large step is closed during the week when access matters most — so seat width, restrainers, approach slabs and well-compacted drained fill are network-level life-safety measures.

    27 · Resilience, honestly costed

    Session 27 · 14 min

    You propose the resilient option and the answer comes back: we cannot afford it. It is a reasonable answer, and it is usually wrong — not because resilience is always worth it, but because the comparison being made is the wrong one. Construction cost is being compared against construction cost, when the resilient option's whole point is that it changes the other columns. Nobody has been shown the other columns, and producing them is a job for an engineer, not an economist.

    The idea

    "Resilient costs more" is a statement about one column of a table. Put the maintenance, the periodic reconstruction and the cost of closure alongside it, and the ranking often reverses — but not always, and knowing when it does not is what makes you credible.

    The four columns

    ColumnWhat it containsWho normally sees it
    CapitalConstruction costEveryone. Usually the only one in the room
    MaintenanceAnnual drain clearing, apron replenishment, vegetation, small repairsA different budget, often a different institution
    ReconstructionThe periodic rebuild — every monsoon, every few years, every decadeCharged to disaster or emergency funds, so it never appears against the original decision
    ClosureWhat it costs the district to have no road: goods not moved, patients not reached, produce spoiled, school missedNobody. It falls on people who were not consulted

    The structural problem is in the right-hand column. Each cost is borne by a different budget, and in most cases by a different institution from the one making the decision. The municipality that builds cheaply does not pay for the emergency reconstruction; the emergency fund that pays for it did not choose the design; and the household that cannot reach a clinic pays a cost that appears in no ledger at all. The decision is rational for the person taking it and wrong for the system. Recognising that is what lets you argue usefully instead of moralising.

    A comparison that fits on one page

    You do not need formal economics. You need four numbers and a horizon:

    1. Capital cost of each option.
    2. Annual maintenance each option requires — realistically, including whether anyone will actually do it.
    3. Expected reconstruction — how often, and how much. Take this from the road's own history, which is the most credible source available and costs nothing.
    4. Days closed per year, and roughly what a day of closure costs the people served.

    Then total over twenty or fifty years. Do not discount elaborately — a simple undiscounted total is transparent, hard to dispute, and adequate to reveal a reversal. If the answer only holds under a particular discount rate, it is not a strong argument anyway.

    Illustrative comparison · one kilometre of hill road

    Two options for the same kilometre. The figures are illustrative — the method is the point, and the inputs should come from your own road's history.

    A · minimum costB · built to standard
    Cut, drainage, protectionBasic cut, unlined drain, no catch drain, spoil side-tippedBenched cut, lined drains, catch drain, mass balanced, bio-engineered
    Capital1.0×1.6×
    Annual maintenance0.03× — nominally, and largely not done0.04×
    Reconstruction0.35× every 3 years0.20× every 12 years
    Days closed per year~25~4

    Twenty-year capital and reconstruction, option A.
    1.0 + (0.35 × 6 rebuilds) = 1.0 + 2.10 = 3.10×

    Twenty-year capital and reconstruction, option B.
    1.6 + (0.20 × 1 rebuild) = 1.80×

    Add maintenance over twenty years.
    A: 0.03 × 20 = 0.60 → total 3.70×
    B: 0.04 × 20 = 0.80 → total 2.60×

    Now the column nobody costs.
    A closes about 25 days a year, B about 4 — roughly 420 additional days of closure over twenty years. Whatever value the district puts on a day of access, it is not zero, and it lands entirely on option A.

    Result: the option that was 60 % more expensive to build is roughly 30 % cheaper over twenty years before closure is counted at all — and closure is where most of the real difference sits.

    Illustrative ratios, not published figures. Replace every number with your own road's record before using this in an argument; the history of the road you are discussing is both the most credible source and the hardest for anyone to dispute.

    When resilience is not worth it

    Be able to say this too, or the analysis above reads as advocacy rather than engineering:

    • Very low traffic, easily bypassed. If the road serves a handful of households with an alternative footpath, the closure column is small and the cheap option may genuinely win.
    • Short design life or a temporary purpose. A haul road for a project that ends in three years should not be built for fifty.
    • Where the resilient option still fails. Spending 1.6× to survive a hazard that will destroy it anyway — a fan crossing that a debris flow will take regardless — is worse than building cheap and expendable. Session 8's conclusion.
    • Where maintenance will certainly not happen. An option whose performance depends on annual work, in a place with no funded maintenance, is not a resilient option. It is an expensive option that will fail on the same schedule as the cheap one. Choose the design that survives neglect.

    That last point is the most useful and the least intuitive. Design for the maintenance regime you will actually get, not the one specified. A slightly cruder solution that tolerates neglect frequently beats a better one that does not.

    Get the closure cost from the people who bear it. You will not find it in a schedule of rates. You will get it in an afternoon by asking in the bazaar: what does it cost when this road is shut, what cannot be sold, what happens to somebody who needs the hospital. The numbers will be rough and they will be far more persuasive to a local decision-maker than anything you calculate — because they are that ward's own numbers, and nobody has ever written them down before.

    Judgment

    Never argue for resilience in the abstract. Build the four-column table for the specific road, using its own failure history for the reconstruction column and the bazaar for the closure column, and put it in front of whoever decides. Where the table says the cheap option wins, say so — that is what makes you believed the next time it does not. And always ask the maintenance question honestly before recommending anything, because a resilient design that assumes work nobody is funded to do is not resilient. It is just more expensive.

    Field assignment

    Take one road section with a repeated failure and build the table:

    1. Find out how many times it has been rebuilt and roughly what each cost. Ask the division or the municipality — they know.
    2. Estimate days closed per year. Ask a driver who uses it.
    3. Ask three people in the nearest bazaar what a week of closure costs them.
    4. Total twenty years against the cost of doing it properly once.

    What you probably found: the reconstruction total already exceeds the cost of building it properly, and nobody had ever added it up because each rebuild was a separate emergency in a separate year with a separate budget line. That sum, on one page, is the most useful document you can produce about that road.

    Check yourself

    1 · Name the four columns, and say which one falls on people who were never consulted.

    Capital, maintenance, reconstruction and closure. Closure — goods not moved, patients not reached, produce spoiled — falls on the people served, appears in no ledger, and is almost never counted.

    2 · Why is the cheap decision often rational for the decision-maker and wrong for the system?

    Because each cost sits with a different budget and often a different institution. The body that builds cheaply does not pay for emergency reconstruction; the emergency fund did not choose the design; and the household bearing the closure cost had no say. The incentives are misaligned, not the arithmetic.

    3 · Where should the reconstruction figures in your comparison come from?

    From the road's own failure history — how often it has actually been rebuilt and at what cost. It is the most credible source available, it costs nothing to obtain, and it is very hard for anyone to dispute, because it is their own record.

    4 · Give two cases where the cheap option genuinely wins.

    Very low traffic with an easy bypass, so the closure column is small; and a short design life or temporary purpose. Also where the resilient option would fail anyway — a fan crossing a debris flow will take regardless, where cheap and expendable is the correct answer.

    5 · Why can a resilient design be the wrong choice where maintenance will not happen?

    Because an option whose performance depends on annual work, in a place with no funded maintenance, will fail on roughly the same schedule as the cheap one while costing more. Design for the maintenance regime you will actually get — a cruder solution that tolerates neglect often beats a better one that does not.

    28 · Roads are the lifeline

    Session 28 · 13 min

    After Gorkha, the helicopters became the story. What actually determined whether a village got help in the first week was almost always simpler and less photogenic: whether a road reached it, or whether a road reached somewhere close enough to walk from. Search teams, medical supplies, tarpaulins, food, and later every sack of cement for reconstruction — all of it moves on roads. The road engineer is the response, several days before anyone in an emergency uniform can do anything at all.

    The idea

    Access is the response capacity. A network's value in a disaster is not the sum of its assets but whether it stays connected — which makes redundancy, not strength, the property that matters most, and it is decided by network decisions nobody frames as risk decisions.

    Thinking in networks rather than assets

    Engineering training is asset-shaped: this bridge, this cut, this culvert. Disaster performance is network-shaped, and the two give different answers.

    Consider two districts with identical total road length and identical construction standards. In the first, every settlement hangs off a single valley road. In the second the same length is arranged with two routes into the district and several cross-links. The second district is dramatically more resilient, and no individual structure in it is any stronger. Resilience was bought with topology, not with concrete.

    Three network properties are worth naming, because once you have the words you start seeing them:

    PropertyQuestion it answers
    Critical linkWhich single element, if lost, disconnects the most people? Every corridor has one, and most districts have never identified theirs
    RedundancyIs there a second way in — any second way, including a poor one? A rough fair-weather track that takes four hours is not a bad alternative; it is the difference between isolated and reachable
    Restoration timeNot "will it survive" but "how fast can it be reopened". A structure that fails and is reinstated in three days may outperform one that survives but sits behind a slide nobody can clear for a month

    What breaks, and in what order

    After a major event the failures arrive in a sequence that is worth expecting:

    1. Slope failures on the approaches — hundreds of them, mostly small, collectively decisive. This is what actually closes hill roads, and it is why sessions 9 and 10 are the operational core of this book.
    2. Loss of individual crossings — bridges and culverts, or more often their approaches, session 26.
    3. Blocked links compounding. The clearing equipment is on the wrong side of a slide. The fuel is in a town you cannot reach. The one loader in the district is committed to the highway. Response capacity itself depends on the network that has just failed.
    4. Second-round failures in the following monsoons, from slopes the event loosened — session 1.

    Note that item three is a coupling, not a failure: the resources that fix the network need the network. A district with one loader positioned at the district headquarters, behind the critical link, has effectively no clearing capacity for whatever lies beyond it.

    What to do with this, before anything happens

    Four things, all cheap, none of them requiring construction:

    1 · Identify the critical links, and write them down. For your district or corridor, list the elements whose loss isolates the most people. It is an afternoon with a map and local knowledge. Most districts have never done it, and the list changes what gets prioritised for inspection, maintenance and strengthening. It also tells you where to preposition equipment.

    2 · Map the alternatives, including bad ones. Old trails, seasonal tracks, river crossings usable in the dry season, the route round through the next district. A response has to know these exist, and the person who knows is usually a local overseer rather than anyone with a map.

    3 · Think about where the equipment sits. One excavator on the far side of the district from the critical link is a plan that fails on the day. Distribution beats capacity.

    4 · Design for reopening, not only for survival. This is the design consequence, and it changes real decisions. A bailey-compatible abutment layout, a causeway that can be scraped clear, an embankment that can be rebuilt with local material, a slide site with room to push material somewhere — all of these convert a month's closure into days. Session 19's graceful failure, at network scale.

    Redundancy is unpopular because it looks like waste. A second route is a road that is not needed on any ordinary day, and it will lose every cost-benefit comparison run on ordinary days. The argument that works is the one from session 27 — put the closure cost in, and add the question nobody asks: what is the cost of this district being unreachable for three weeks? That number is not small, and it is the only column in which redundancy ever pays.

    Judgment

    Learn to answer one question about any corridor you work on: which single failure isolates the most people, and is there any other way to them? If you can name the critical link and the alternative, you are ahead of almost every district in the country, and you can direct maintenance, inspection and equipment accordingly. Then push, wherever you have influence, for the cheap network measures — a second link kept passable, equipment distributed rather than centralised, and structures designed to be reopened. Those buy more disaster performance per rupee than strengthening anything.

    Field assignment

    Take your district, or one corridor, and draw it — badly is fine, a sketch on one sheet:

    1. Mark the settlements and the roads connecting them.
    2. Mark every point you consider vulnerable — known slide sites, major crossings.
    3. For each, ask: who is cut off if this goes? Write the number.
    4. Circle the worst. Then find out whether any alternative route exists to those people, by asking someone local.

    What you probably found: one link with a far larger number beside it than the rest, and an alternative that exists but is not on any map and is known only to people who walk. Both findings are valuable, and the second is the kind of thing that belongs in a district preparedness plan and almost never is.

    Check yourself

    1 · Two districts have identical road length and construction standards, but very different disaster performance. What differs?

    Topology. One has every settlement hanging off a single valley road; the other has two routes in and several cross-links. Resilience was bought with network arrangement, not with stronger structures — no individual asset is any better.

    2 · Why is restoration time sometimes more important than survival?

    Because a structure that fails and is reinstated in three days can outperform one that survives but sits behind a slide nobody can clear for a month. What matters to the people beyond it is when access returns, not whether a particular element stood.

    3 · Explain the coupling between response capacity and the network.

    The resources that repair the network depend on the network. Clearing equipment ends up on the wrong side of a slide, fuel is in an unreachable town, and the district's single loader is committed elsewhere — so a district with its equipment behind the critical link has effectively no capacity beyond it.

    4 · Why should poor alternative routes be mapped rather than dismissed?

    Because a rough fair-weather track taking four hours is the difference between isolated and reachable. Responders need to know it exists, and that knowledge usually sits with a local overseer rather than on any map — which is exactly why it should be written into a preparedness plan.

    5 · Redundancy loses every ordinary cost-benefit comparison. What is the argument that works?

    Add the closure column, then ask what it costs for this district to be unreachable for three weeks. Redundancy only ever pays in that column — on ordinary days a second route is a road nobody needs, which is precisely why it never survives an ordinary-day appraisal.

    29 · The first 72 hours

    Session 29 · 14 min

    The road engineer's disaster job begins before anyone has decided there is a disaster. While the district is still counting, you are the person who can answer the question everything else depends on: what can we reach, and by when? Nobody will formally give you that job. You will simply be the only person in the room who knows the network, and the quality of the first three days will turn substantially on whether you can answer quickly and honestly rather than accurately and late.

    The idea

    In the first 72 hours your product is not repair — it is information and triage. A rough, fast, honest picture of what is passable, what can be opened quickly and what cannot be opened at all is worth more than any amount of clearing done in the wrong order.

    Hour zero to twelve: find out

    The first task is reconnaissance, and the trap is waiting for good information. You will not get it. Work with what arrives:

    • Phone the overseers and the ward offices. The people on the road know within an hour what is blocked. This is faster than any survey and more reliable than early reports through official channels.
    • Ask a specific question. Not "how is the road" but "can a loaded truck get from X to Y, yes or no, and if no, where does it stop?" That single formulation turns vague reports into a map.
    • Record where each route stops. The stopping points are the work list, and the distance beyond them is what determines whether a settlement is reachable on foot.
    • Report early and label the confidence. A rough picture at hour six, marked as rough, is far more useful than a verified one at hour forty-eight.

    Triage: the order matters more than the effort

    You will have less equipment than blockages. Clearing in the wrong order is the most common and least visible failure of the first days. Sort every blockage by two questions — how many people does opening it reach, and how long will it take — and do the cheap, high-value ones first.

    Reaches manyReaches few
    Quick to openFirst. Always, and often overlooked because they seem trivialSecond — cheap wins that free the network
    Slow to openThird — start it, but do not let it absorb everything. Look for a bypass firstLast. Consider walking access or air instead

    Two habits that repeatedly pay: look for a bypass before committing to a clearance — a track through a field, a river terrace, an old alignment can restore access in hours where clearing the slide would take a week. And open one lane, not two. A single passable lane serves the response; the second lane can wait until the emergency phase is over.

    The engineer's specific tasks

    Assess before you clear. A slide that is still moving will kill people working beneath it, and the pressure to get machines in is intense. Session 9's signs, read in ten minutes: fresh cracks above the crown, material still arriving, water emerging. You are the only person present who is qualified to stop that work, and it is worth knowing in advance that you may have to.

    Check crossings before loading them. A bridge that looks intact may have lost its approach fill, moved on its bearings, or been scoured. A quick structural triage — seating, abutment position, obvious settlement, the bed around the piers — before the first loaded truck goes over. Session 26's four interfaces, in ten minutes each.

    Watch for the second event. After an earthquake, slopes keep failing. After heavy rain, they fail for days. And if a slide has blocked a river anywhere upstream, session 5 applies immediately: that is a lake filling above a population, and somebody has to raise it now rather than next week.

    Temporary crossings. Know what is available before you need it — a bailey-type panel bridge in the district or region, a suitable ford site, a river narrow enough for a temporary span. Establishing the location of the nearest bailey set is a five-minute task on an ordinary day and an impossible one on the day itself.

    How the response is organised around you

    You will be working inside a structure, and knowing its shape saves arguments:

    • The District and Local Disaster Management Committees from session 4 are the coordinating bodies, chaired by the CDO and the local executive respectively. That is where your information should go and where tasking should come from.
    • The Nepal Army, Armed Police Force and Nepal Police carry most search, rescue and heavy clearance capacity. Your value to them is knowing where to send it.
    • Aid organisations work in clusters by sector. Roads and access sit with the government's own infrastructure arrangements rather than with a humanitarian cluster, which is exactly why the engineer's link into the district committee matters.
    • An Initial Rapid Assessment is done in the first days to size the response; a fuller assessment follows later. Access information is one of its most important inputs, and it is frequently the weakest.

    Write down what you did and what you saw. In the first days nobody has time to record anything, and three months later the recovery programme will be built on whatever was written. Photographs with locations, a list of failures, the dates routes reopened — kept in a notebook, they become the evidence base for reconstruction funding and, more importantly, for arguing that a rebuilt road should be aligned differently. Almost nobody does this, and the ones who do end up shaping what gets rebuilt.

    Judgment

    In the first hours, favour speed and honesty over precision: a rough access picture labelled rough, delivered at hour six to the district committee, is the highest-value thing you can produce. Then triage ruthlessly — quick wins that reach many people first, bypasses before clearances, one lane not two. And hold one line without apology: nobody works under a slide that is still moving. You will be the only person qualified to make that call, and the pressure to make it the other way will be considerable.

    Field assignment

    Prepare, on one page, what you would need on the day. For your district or corridor:

    1. Phone numbers — overseers, ward technical staff, the division office, the local contractor with an excavator.
    2. Equipment — what plant exists in the district, and where does it normally sit?
    3. Temporary crossing — where is the nearest bailey set or equivalent?
    4. Your critical link from session 28, and its alternative.

    What you probably found: that two of those four took a while to establish, and that nobody had them written in one place. That page — kept in a drawer and updated once a year — is genuine preparedness, and it costs an afternoon.

    Check yourself

    1 · What is the engineer's actual product in the first 72 hours?

    Information and triage — a fast, rough, honest picture of what is passable, what can be opened quickly and what cannot. That is worth more than clearing done in the wrong order, and it is available far sooner than repair.

    2 · What is the right question to ask an overseer by phone, and why that form?

    "Can a loaded truck get from X to Y — yes or no — and if no, where does it stop?" It converts vague damage reports into a map, gives the work list directly from the stopping points, and can be answered immediately by someone standing on the road.

    3 · Give the triage rule and the two habits that go with it.

    Sort blockages by how many people opening them reaches and how long they take; do the quick, high-value ones first, and leave slow low-value ones last. The habits: look for a bypass before committing to a clearance, and open one lane rather than two.

    4 · What is the one call only the engineer can make on site?

    Stopping work beneath a slide that is still moving — fresh crown cracks, material still arriving, water emerging. The pressure to keep machines working will be intense, and nobody else present is qualified to judge it.

    5 · Why does keeping a notebook in the first days matter months later?

    Because the recovery programme gets built on whatever was written down, and almost nothing is. Located photographs, a list of failures and reopening dates become the evidence base for reconstruction funding — and for arguing that a rebuilt road should follow a different alignment.

    30 · Buildings, compressed

    Session 30 · core · 16 min

    You are a road engineer, and one day somebody will put a school in front of you. A ward will ask whether the health post is safe. A committee will need a member who can read a building. You do not need to become a structural designer — but in a country where the next great earthquake is a certainty, an engineer who cannot say anything useful about a building is missing a part of the job that other people assume you have. This session is the whole building world compressed to what you can actually use: enough to judge a school, hold an argument, and know when to call somebody else.

    The idea

    Buildings fail in a small number of recognisable ways, most of them visible from the street. Learn the four killers and the load path, and you can form a defensible opinion about most Nepali buildings in ten minutes — and know precisely when you must not.

    What an earthquake does to a building

    The ground moves; the building's mass resists moving; the difference is an inertia force, proportional to mass, applied at every floor. It travels down through the structure to the foundation, and the path it takes is the whole of seismic design:

    floor mass → slab → beams → columns or walls → foundation → ground

    Break that chain anywhere and everything above the break is unsupported. Three properties of the chain matter:

    • Continuity. Every element must be connected to the next. A floor not tied to its walls, a wall not tied to the roof — these are the commonest defects in non-engineered construction.
    • Diaphragm action. The floor must be stiff enough in its own plane to distribute the force among the vertical elements. A stiff RC slab does this; loose timber planks on joists do not, so each wall is left to fend for itself.
    • Ductility. The ability to deform substantially without losing strength. It is what buys the time for people to get out. A brittle structure at its limit fails without warning; a ductile one cracks, sags, and stands.

    The four killers you can see from the street

    1 · Soft storey. A ground floor left open for shops or parking while the floors above are stiffened by masonry infill. All the seismic drift concentrates in the one flexible level, and the columns there must accommodate the whole displacement. This is the single most lethal configuration defect in Nepal, it is visible from across the street, and it is being built today because ground-floor shop frontage is valuable.

    2 · Short column. A column whose clear height is reduced by partial infill — a masonry wall part-way up, or a sloping site with columns of unequal length. A short column is stiffer, so it attracts more force, and it has less height over which to deform. It fails in brittle shear rather than ductile bending, often explosively. Look for ventilation-height masonry between columns.

    3 · Plan and vertical irregularity. Where stiffness is concentrated to one side — a staircase core at one end, a solid party wall along one edge — the building twists about that stiff point, and the elements furthest from it are dragged through much larger displacements. Also watch for heavy upper floors, setbacks, and floating columns that stop part-way down.

    4 · Pounding. Adjacent buildings without a separation gap, particularly with floors at different levels, so one building's slab strikes the other's column mid-height. Very common in Nepali town cores, where buildings share walls by default.

    The code, in outline

    Nepal's National Building Code is enforced by municipalities through the building permit process under the Building Act. Two parts are worth knowing by name:

    • NBC 105 — Seismic Design of Buildings in Nepal. Substantially revised in 2020 from the 1994 original, bringing it into line with modern practice: an updated seismic hazard basis, explicit performance objectives with separate ultimate and serviceability checks, and modern ductility and site-class treatment. If somebody cites a design to "NBC 105", ask which edition — the difference is large.
    • The Mandatory Rules of Thumb and the guidelines for non-engineered construction. These give buildable rules — member sizes, reinforcement, wall lengths — for small buildings without an engineer's analysis, which is what most Nepali housing actually is.

    Masonry, which is most of Nepal

    Most housing in the hills is stone or brick in mud mortar, and it fails in ways that have nothing to do with frames. The rules that keep it standing are few and worth knowing, because they are cheap and constantly omitted:

    • Horizontal bands — continuous reinforced bands at plinth, sill, lintel and roof level, tying the walls into a box. Lintel band is the most important single one.
    • Vertical reinforcement at corners and at openings.
    • Through-stones in stone masonry, tying the two wythes together so the wall does not split down its middle and shed its outer leaf.
    • Gable band and roof tie, so the gable does not fall outwards.
    • Limits on wall length, height and opening size — long unrestrained walls fail out of plane.

    Jajarkot in 2023 is the case study: a moderate earthquake, and lethal largely because of mud-mortar stone housing without any of the above.

    Know the edge of your competence, and say where it is. You can identify configuration defects, missing bands, obvious deterioration and dangerous alterations, and you can say a building needs proper assessment. You cannot certify a structure, design a retrofit, or declare a school safe — those need a structural engineer with the drawings and the material data. The useful contribution is a clear, written, non-technical flag: "this building has an open ground floor with no shear walls and requires assessment before it is used as an emergency shelter." That sentence is within your competence and it is worth a great deal.

    Judgment

    Walk round the outside and check five things: is the ground floor open, are any columns shortened by partial infill, is the stiffness lopsided in plan, does it touch its neighbours, and — for masonry — can you see bands at lintel level. That is ten minutes and it will correctly flag most of the dangerous buildings you meet. Then write down what you saw, say plainly that it needs proper assessment, and give it to the municipality. Do not be pushed into certifying anything. And if you take one number from this session, take the question "which edition of NBC 105?" — it separates a current design from a thirty-year-old one.

    Field assignment

    Walk one street of your nearest bazaar and score every building on the four killers:

    1. Open ground floor?
    2. Any columns shortened by partial infill?
    3. Obvious plan irregularity, or a heavy stiff element at one end?
    4. Touching its neighbour, with floors at different levels?

    Then pick the single building you would least like to be in, and write one sentence saying why.

    What you probably found: nearly every commercial building has an open ground floor, most touch their neighbours, and the newest buildings are not obviously better than the older ones. That is the ordinary condition of a Nepali bazaar, and it is why the building stock question is a permit-enforcement question rather than a design question.

    Check yourself

    1 · Trace the load path for an inertia force, and name the three properties the chain needs.

    Floor mass → slab → beams → columns or walls → foundation → ground. It needs continuity (every element tied to the next), diaphragm action (the floor stiff enough in plane to share the force among vertical elements), and ductility (deforming substantially without losing strength).

    2 · Why is a soft storey so lethal, and why does it keep being built?

    Because the floors above are stiffened by infill while the open ground floor is not, so the entire seismic drift concentrates in that one level and its columns must absorb the whole displacement. It keeps being built because open ground-floor frontage is commercially valuable.

    3 · Why is a short column dangerous rather than simply stronger?

    Being shorter it is stiffer, so it attracts more force, while having less height over which to deform. It therefore fails in brittle shear rather than ductile bending — often suddenly. Partial infill at ventilation height is the usual cause.

    4 · Name the masonry rules that keep mud-mortar housing standing.

    Continuous horizontal bands at plinth, sill, lintel and roof — lintel band most important; vertical reinforcement at corners and openings; through-stones tying the wythes so the wall does not split; a gable band and roof tie; and limits on wall length, height and opening size.

    5 · Where is the edge of a road engineer's competence here, and what is the useful contribution?

    You can identify configuration defects, missing bands, deterioration and dangerous alterations, and say a building needs assessment. You cannot certify a structure, design a retrofit or declare a school safe. The valuable output is a clear written flag — for example that a building has an open ground floor with no shear walls and must be assessed before being used as a shelter.

    31 · Building back better

    Session 31 · 14 min

    After 2015, Nepal did something that most countries do not manage: it rebuilt something in the order of eight hundred thousand houses, and it did so by giving the money to the owners and inspecting the work in stages rather than by hiring contractors to build for them. It was slow, it was criticised constantly, and by the standard that matters — houses that will survive the next one — it largely worked. Roads were the part that went less well, and the reason is worth understanding, because it will repeat.

    The idea

    Reconstruction is the one moment when risk can be substantially reduced, because everything is being rebuilt anyway. It is also the moment of maximum pressure to rebuild exactly what was there — and speed and risk reduction pull in opposite directions in a way nobody says out loud.

    What "build back better" actually requires

    The phrase is in every document and it means very little unless it is unpacked into decisions somebody has to take:

    1. Rebuild to a better standard — the easy part, and the part that gets done. Compliance with the code, with the detail, with the specification.
    2. Rebuild in a better place — the hard part, and the part that mostly does not happen. If the school was destroyed by a landslide, rebuilding it on the same plot to a higher standard is not risk reduction.
    3. Rebuild a better system — hardest of all. Redundancy, maintenance capacity, funded institutions.

    Nearly all the achievement of any reconstruction programme is in the first, and nearly all the remaining risk is in the second and third.

    What the housing programme got right

    The NRA's approach was owner-driven: a grant paid in tranches, released against inspection at defined stages, with technical guidance and trained masons rather than contractors. Four things about it are worth carrying:

    • Tranche release tied to inspection put a technical person on site at the moments that matter — foundation, bands, roof. That is where compliance is actually won.
    • Mason training at scale put the knowledge into the districts permanently, and it stays after the programme ends. This may be the most durable achievement of the whole exercise.
    • Owner control meant houses that suited how people live, and were finished, and were maintained — the standard failure of contractor-built resettlement housing is that nobody moves in.
    • Catalogue designs gave buildable options rather than requiring an engineer for every house.

    The honest criticisms are also real: it was slow, the grant did not cover a full house so households borrowed, the most vulnerable found compliance hardest, and inspection quality varied. But the shape of the thing was sound, and it is the model to argue for next time.

    Why roads did worse

    Three structural reasons, and none is an accident:

    HousingRoads
    Each house is a discrete unit with an owner who wants it to surviveA road has no owner with a personal stake in its durability
    Standard designs and trained masons could be scaledEvery road section is site-specific — alignment, geology, drainage — and cannot be catalogued
    Compliance was inspectable at defined stagesThe critical decisions — alignment, spoil, drainage — are taken during construction and are invisible afterwards

    Add the emergency imperative, and you get the pattern this book keeps returning to: a severed road is reinstated fast, on the same alignment, by pushing material off the edge, because that reopens it this week. Every one of those decisions is defensible in isolation and all of them rebuild the risk. Reconstruction is when the mistakes of session 10 get made at scale and at speed, funded by money that is available precisely because of the disaster.

    The engineer's opening, and it is narrow

    There is a window — roughly between the emergency phase ending and the reconstruction programme fixing its designs — in which alignment decisions can be reopened. It is short, it closes quietly, and using it needs preparation done beforehand:

    1. Have the evidence ready. Session 29's notebook. If you can show that this section has failed four times in twelve years, at these locations, the case for realignment writes itself. Nobody argues with their own record.
    2. Have the alternative already sketched. "Realign it" invites delay; "here is a corridor that avoids the three recurring failure sites, adding 900 m" invites a decision.
    3. Use the assessment process. Post-disaster needs assessments size the reconstruction budget. What is written into them becomes what gets funded, and "reinstate to previous condition" versus "reinstate with realignment at three sites" is a distinction settled in a document, months before any machine moves.
    4. Separate emergency reinstatement from permanent reconstruction, in writing. Reopen the road on the old line if you must, but record explicitly that this is temporary and that the permanent works require a decision. Otherwise the temporary becomes the permanent by default, which is the single most common way risk gets rebuilt.

    Reconstruction money is the largest budget your district will ever see, and it is spent fastest at the moment it is least well considered. The pressure to disburse is enormous and it is not irrational — people are living under tarpaulins. But a great deal of what looks like disaster damage is actually the previous reconstruction, and pointing that out early, politely, with photographs, is one of the more valuable things an engineer can do. Say it before the programme's designs are frozen; afterwards it reads as criticism rather than input.

    Judgment

    Treat reconstruction as a design opportunity with a deadline, and prepare for it before the event. Keep the failure record for the roads you are responsible for, so that when the window opens you have evidence rather than opinion. Push hardest on the second question — should this be rebuilt in the same place? — because that is where the risk actually sits, and it is the one nobody raises. And always insist that emergency reinstatement is recorded as temporary. The distinction between temporary and permanent is the difference between reopening a road and rebuilding a hazard.

    Field assignment

    Find a road section that has been rebuilt at least twice after failures. Then:

    1. Establish, by asking, how many times and roughly when.
    2. Look at whether anything changed each time — alignment, drainage, protection — or whether it was reinstated as it was.
    3. Sketch one alternative that avoids the failure site, with its rough extra length.
    4. Write four sentences: what fails, how often, what it costs, what the alternative is.

    What you probably found: repeated reinstatement on the same line with nothing changed, and an alternative that was always available. Those four sentences are exactly what you will need on the day the next reconstruction budget appears — and writing them now, when there is no pressure, is what makes them usable then.

    Check yourself

    1 · Unpack "build back better" into its three requirements, and say where the achievement and the remaining risk sit.

    Rebuild to a better standard, in a better place, and as a better system. Nearly all the achievement of any programme is in the first; nearly all the residual risk is in the second and third. Rebuilding a landslide-destroyed school on the same plot to a higher standard is not risk reduction.

    2 · What were the durable strengths of the owner-driven housing model?

    Grant tranches released against staged inspection, which put a technical person on site at foundation, band and roof level; mason training at scale, which left knowledge permanently in the districts; owner control, so houses suited how people live and were occupied and maintained; and catalogue designs that removed the need for an engineer per house.

    3 · Give the three structural reasons road reconstruction went worse than housing.

    A road has no owner with a personal stake in its durability; every section is site-specific so it cannot be catalogued or scaled the way house designs were; and its critical decisions — alignment, spoil, drainage — are taken during construction and are invisible afterwards, so they cannot be inspected at defined stages.

    4 · Why is the reconstruction window so short, and what must be prepared in advance?

    It sits between the emergency phase ending and the programme freezing its designs, and it closes quietly. You need the failure record already compiled and an alternative corridor already sketched — because "realign it" invites delay while a specific alternative with its extra length invites a decision.

    5 · Why insist in writing that emergency reinstatement is temporary?

    Because otherwise the temporary becomes permanent by default — the road is reopened on the old line by pushing material over the edge, and that reinstatement is never revisited. Recording it as temporary preserves the requirement for a permanent decision, and that distinction is the difference between reopening a road and rebuilding a hazard.

    32 · Land, money, the next one

    Session 32 · 14 min

    Every session in this book has been about reducing the risk of something that already exists — a slope you have cut, a crossing you must build, a road you must reopen. This one is about the risk that has not been created yet, and it turns on three unglamorous things that no engineer controls but every engineer influences: where people are allowed to build, who pays for maintenance, and who carries the loss. Get those wrong and everything else in this book is applied to a problem that keeps getting bigger.

    The idea

    The cheapest risk reduction available to any country is not building in the wrong place to begin with. The second cheapest is maintaining what exists. Both are decided by institutions and money rather than by engineering — which is precisely why an engineer's voice in those rooms is worth more than another design.

    Risk-sensitive land use planning

    The idea is simple: know where the hazard is, and steer development away from it. In practice three things have to line up, and in Nepal usually two do.

    • The information. Susceptibility mapping — session 12, with its limitations honestly stated. This mostly exists now.
    • The instrument. Local governments have planning and building permit powers. This exists too.
    • The will, and the alternative. This is what is usually missing — because restricting development means telling somebody they cannot build on land they own, and unless there is somewhere else for them to go, the restriction is not a plan. It is a hardship.

    The engineer's honest contribution here is narrower than the rhetoric suggests, and it is worth doing well:

    1. Get the hazard information in front of the planning decision at the moment it is being taken, not afterwards. A municipality expanding a settlement usually has no hazard input at all.
    2. Distinguish "unbuildable" from "buildable with conditions." Blanket red zones get ignored. "Not on the fan below the apex, and anything on this terrace needs the drainage taken to the khola" is a rule people can follow.
    3. Protect the cheap wins. Keeping new public buildings — schools, health posts, government offices — off hazardous ground is achievable even where private land use cannot be controlled, and it protects the buildings that must function after an event.

    Maintenance: the risk reduction nobody funds

    Every session in Act IV ended in the same place. The catch drain works if it is cleared. The apron works if it is replenished. The bio-engineering works if it is protected from grazing. The culvert works if the inlet is cleaned before the monsoon. Maintenance is the highest-return risk reduction available on an existing network, and it is structurally the hardest thing to fund for reasons that are political rather than technical:

    New constructionMaintenance
    Visible. There is a ribbonInvisible. Success looks like nothing happening
    Attributable to whoever funded itAttributable to nobody
    Attracts capital budgets and donorsCompetes with salaries in a recurrent budget
    Failure is somebody else's problem laterFailure is immediate and blamed on the last repair

    Three things that actually help, in ascending order of ambition: attach a named maintenance responsibility and a schedule to every handover, so the absence is at least recorded; make the pre-monsoon inspection an event with a date rather than an intention; and where you can influence budgets, argue for maintenance in the language of session 27 — not as a good habit but as the alternative to reconstruction, with the district's own reconstruction history as the evidence.

    Who carries the loss

    When a hazard becomes a disaster, somebody pays. In Nepal it is overwhelmingly the affected household, from savings and borrowing, followed by government relief. Two mechanisms shift that, and both are relevant to your work:

    Disaster funds. The DRRM Act framework establishes disaster management funds at each tier of government. Their existence matters less than what they are permitted to spend on: a fund that can only be spent on relief and reconstruction pays for damage, while one that can be spent on prevention — a retaining wall before the slide, a drain before the monsoon — buys risk reduction. Where local funds allow preventive spending, an engineer with a costed, evidenced proposal is unusually well placed, because most such funds go unspent for want of a specific proposal rather than for want of money.

    Insurance. Thin, and thinner in the hills. Crop and livestock insurance schemes exist with subsidy; property catastrophe cover is essentially absent for rural households. Insurance transfers loss rather than reducing it, and it only prices risk correctly where the risk is known — which loops back to the hazard information above.

    Closing the loop

    Return to session 2. Risk is hazard, exposure and vulnerability over capacity. Everything in Acts II to IV worked on vulnerability — how a slope, a crossing, a road behaves when the hazard arrives. This session is about the other two terms:

    • Land use is exposure. The only term that can be reduced to zero, and the only one that gets cheaper the earlier it is addressed.
    • Maintenance, funding and redundancy are capacity. The term that determines whether everything else survives contact with reality.

    Which produces the one sentence this whole handbook has been building towards: the engineering is necessary and it is not sufficient — and the engineer who understands the other terms, and can argue them in a municipality office with the district's own numbers, is worth several who can only design.

    Do not mistake this for someone else's job. Planning officers, committees and finance people do not have the hazard knowledge, and will not acquire it. You do. The distance between a susceptibility map sitting in a drawer and a decision not to extend a settlement onto a fan is usually one engineer who showed up to the right meeting with one page. That is a low bar and it is very rarely met.

    Judgment

    Spend part of your effort outside engineering, where the leverage is larger. Get hazard information into planning decisions before they are taken, and phrase it as conditions rather than prohibitions so it can actually be applied. Fight for public buildings to be sited safely even where you cannot influence private land. Attach a named, dated maintenance responsibility to everything you hand over. And find out what your local disaster fund is permitted to spend on — if it allows prevention, a costed proposal backed by the failure record is one of the few places where an engineer can convert knowledge directly into risk reduction.

    Field assignment

    Two questions for your own municipality, both answerable by asking:

    1. Is any hazard information used when building permits or settlement expansion are decided? If yes, what and by whom. If no — and it usually is no — what would it take?
    2. Does the local disaster management fund exist, what is in it, and can it be spent on prevention rather than only on relief?

    Then pick one site you know is hazardous and drafted as a single page: the hazard, the exposure, what it would cost to reduce, and what it will cost if nobody does.

    What you probably found: no hazard input to permits, and a fund with money in it that nobody has proposed anything specific for. That combination is an open door, and the single page is what walks through it.

    Check yourself

    1 · Three things must line up for risk-sensitive land use planning. Which is usually missing in Nepal, and why?

    The information exists and the local planning instrument exists; what is missing is the will and the alternative. Restricting development means telling someone they cannot build on land they own, and without somewhere else to go that is not a plan but a hardship.

    2 · Why do blanket red zones fail, and what works better?

    They get ignored, because they are absolute and offer no path to compliance. Conditions work better — not on the fan below the apex, and anything on this terrace must take its drainage to the khola. A rule people can follow is applied; a prohibition is evaded.

    3 · Give two structural reasons maintenance is harder to fund than new construction.

    Maintenance is invisible — success looks like nothing happening — and it is attributable to nobody, whereas new construction is visible and credited to whoever funded it. It also competes inside a recurrent budget rather than attracting capital and donor money.

    4 · What matters most about a local disaster fund, beyond its size?

    What it is permitted to spend on. A fund limited to relief and reconstruction pays for damage; one that can fund prevention — a wall before the slide, a drain before the monsoon — buys risk reduction. Many such funds go unspent for want of a specific costed proposal rather than for want of money.

    5 · Restate the closing argument in terms of session 2's four terms.

    Acts II to IV worked on vulnerability. Land use is exposure — the only term reducible to zero and the cheapest to address early. Maintenance, funding and redundancy are capacity — the term that decides whether everything else survives contact with reality. The engineering is necessary and not sufficient.

    Field checklists

    Four checklists, harvested from the sessions. They are written to be used standing up, on site, with a phone in one hand — so each item is something you can actually see or ask, not something you have to calculate.

    A · Slope inspection

    From sessions 6, 9 and 10. Work from the crown down; the road is at the toe and the toe speaks last.

    1. Stand back first — from across the valley if you can. Amphitheatre shape? Mid-slope bulge? Hummocky ground among smooth? Different vegetation?
    2. Crown — tension cracks, arcuate, roughly along the contour. Measure the width. Mark and date them.
    3. Scarp — fresh and bare, or rounded and vegetated? Height ≈ displacement so far.
    4. Things that lean — trees, poles, terrace risers. Back into the slope = rotational. Downslope or bent at base = translational or creep.
    5. Straight lines that stopped being straight — terraces, walls, irrigation channels, road crest.
    6. Water — new, moved or dried springs; seepage surviving the dry season; a line of greener vegetation. Note the elevation of any seepage line.
    7. Toe — heave, bulging, a wall leaning out, a drain that has lost its fall.
    8. Where does water enter from above? Road drain, irrigation channel, diverted khola, crown crack.
    9. Depth judgement — shallow (under ~3 m) or deep-seated? This decides whether any remedy you propose can reach it.
    10. Leave a datum — paint marks across a crack with the gap and date, and a photo from a findable point with something permanent in frame.
    11. Ask a farmer — has any spring here changed in your lifetime?

    B · Hill road drainage walk

    From sessions 21 and 22. One kilometre, on foot, with a sketch.

    1. Catch drain above the cut — present or absent? (Usually absent.)
    2. Side drain — lined or unlined? Does it have fall? Is it silted?
    3. Turnouts — count them, note the spacing. Long unrelieved stretches?
    4. Walk to every outlet. What receives the water — lined chute, natural channel, or open hillside? Photograph each.
    5. Camber — intact, flat, or reversed? Is water running along the carriageway?
    6. Culverts — see checklist C.
    7. Spoil — anywhere pushed over the edge? How far down does the disturbance reach?
    8. Failures — mark every one, above and below the road, with its distance from the nearest outlet or spoil tip.
    9. Overlay. How many failures sit within 50 m downhill of an outlet?
    10. Ask — who clears these before the monsoon, and are they paid?

    C · Culvert and cross-drainage

    From session 22. Five lines each; do five culverts and the pattern appears.

    1. Size and type — and could a person get inside to clean it?
    2. Inlet — clear, partly blocked, fully blocked? Headwall and wing walls?
    3. Outlet — apron present? Cut-off wall? Scour hole? Chute to a channel?
    4. Barrel — silt or debris standing in it (insufficient fall or drowned outlet)? Cracked or separated joints?
    5. Upstream — ponding, sediment fan, evidence of afflux?
    6. Fill above — damp patch or settlement over the barrel? That is piping, and it is serious.
    7. The question — if this blocked completely, where would the water go?

    D · Bridge, from the ground

    From sessions 17 and 26. Inspect below the bearing line — that is where bridges are lost.

    1. Approach embankments, both ends — what are they tied into? Any erosion starting behind the abutment? Outflanking is the classic loss.
    2. Settlement step at the abutment — how large? Would a loaded truck cross it?
    3. Bearings and shelf — can you see them at all? Seat width beyond the bearing? Seized bearings, blocked expansion joints?
    4. Piers — bed level against a fixed reference on the pier (construction joint, stain line, top of footing).
    5. Apron — intact, displaced, partly launched, or gone? Photograph it.
    6. Probe the bed beside a pier with a rod, and away from it. Does it go in further beside the pier? That is refilled scour.
    7. Deck drainage — where does it discharge? Onto the abutment backfill?
    8. Training works — guide bunds, spurs. Toe protection? Ends keyed in?
    9. Gravel extraction anywhere near, upstream or down?
    10. Records — is there a bed profile from previous years at the same sections? If not, start one.

    E · The day-one page

    From sessions 28 and 29. Fill this in on an ordinary day; it is impossible to assemble on the day you need it.

    • Phone numbers — overseers, ward technical staff, division office, the local contractor with an excavator.
    • Plant — what exists in the district, and where does it normally sit?
    • Temporary crossing — location of the nearest bailey set or equivalent.
    • Critical link — the single element whose loss isolates the most people. And the number of people.
    • Alternatives — every other way in, including bad ones: old trails, seasonal tracks, dry-season fords, the route round through the next district.
    • Upstream — lakes, narrow reaches that could be dammed by a slide, and who would need to be told.

    Act I — The Ground

    1 · Far-western Nepal has had no great earthquake since 1505. What does that imply?

    The segment is aseismic and does not store strain
    The risk there is lower than in areas that ruptured recently
    Five centuries of convergence is stored there as unreleased slip
    The historical record is simply incomplete

    The thrust is locked while India converges at ~18–20 mm/yr, so quiet time means accumulating strain, not absence of hazard. Option B inverts the logic and is the belief you will most often have to argue against.

    2 · For a road engineer in Nepal, how does a large earthquake principally arrive?

    As ground shaking that overloads structural members
    As tens of thousands of simultaneous landslides
    As liquefaction across the Terai
    As permanent fault displacement across alignments

    Gorkha triggered on the order of 25,000 landslides. The network is cut in hundreds of places at once, so clearing plant cannot reach most of them. Option A is what building-focused training prepares you for and is not the dominant road failure mode.

    3 · A hillside with a very high landslide susceptibility and no people or assets on it has:

    High risk, because the hazard is high
    Moderate risk, pending assessment
    High vulnerability
    Essentially no risk

    Risk requires exposure. With nothing exposed the product is zero however severe the hazard — which is exactly why a susceptibility map is not a risk map.

    4 · Which term in the risk relation can a road engineer sometimes increase?

    Hazard
    Exposure only
    Capacity only
    None — hazard is fixed by nature

    Cutting a slope, tipping spoil and concentrating drainage manufacture hazard where none existed. Option D is the textbook answer and is wrong in hill road work — which is the central uncomfortable fact of this book.

    5 · Gentle, terraced, long-cultivated ground in the Middle Hills is:

    Reliably stable, as its history demonstrates
    Unsuitable for roads because of the terraces
    Frequently dormant colluvium — an old slide that has been quiet
    Typically shallow soil over sound rock

    Much of it is old landslide debris. It is dormant, not stable, and cutting through its toe is a reliable way to reactivate it. Option A is the intuition that gets alignments into trouble.

    6 · A slide has just dammed a river upstream of your works. This is:

    A geological event to be assessed in the next survey cycle
    An active emergency — landslide dams often fail within days or weeks
    Beneficial, because it reduces downstream flood peaks
    Only a concern if the dam exceeds 20 m in height

    The impoundment, its filling rate, who is downstream and whether anyone is watching are immediate questions. Jure is the case: the slide killed 156 and the dam threatened a whole valley for weeks afterwards.

    Act II — Reading the Terrain

    7 · Rain reduces slope stability mainly because it:

    Adds significant weight to the slope
    Lubricates the slip surface
    Raises pore pressure, reducing effective stress and frictional strength
    Softens the rock mass

    Friction is mobilised by (σ − u), not σ. Options A and B are the two most common lay explanations and both are essentially wrong — the slope does not get meaningfully heavier or more slippery, it gets weaker.

    8 · A vertical cut in silty soil has stood through two dry seasons. This shows:

    The design cut angle is adequate
    The soil has genuine cohesion
    The slope has consolidated and gained strength
    Nothing — it is standing on matric suction, which saturation removes

    Apparent cohesion from partly saturated pores disappears completely on saturation. The face was never strong; it was temporarily held, and the first heavy monsoon is the real test.

    9 · Why is a factor of safety meaningful for a slide but not for a flow?

    A slide moves on a definable shear surface; a flow shears internally throughout
    Flows are always faster, so equilibrium does not apply
    Flow material has no measurable shear strength
    Factors of safety apply only to rock

    Limit equilibrium needs a surface on which to compare resisting and driving forces. A flow has none, so it is a routing and volume problem instead.

    10 · A gully above your alignment has not run in thirty years. This is:

    Reassuring — the catchment is evidently stable
    Concerning — it is loaded with thirty years of stored debris
    Neutral, since debris flows are random events
    Evidence that upstream slopes have stabilised

    Quiet years accumulate rockfall, bank collapse and slide debris in the channel bed. That stored material is what a future flow entrains, and it sets the volume that arrives at your road.

    11 · Trees on a slope tilt backwards, into the hill. This indicates:

    Wind loading
    Translational sliding
    Rotational movement
    Soil creep only

    A mass rotating on a curved surface tips its upper portion back towards the slope. Downslope tilt, or a bent base with a straight upper trunk, indicates translational movement or creep instead — the direction is diagnostic.

    12 · A roadside drain silts up repeatedly for no evident reason. Check first:

    Whether the contractor used the wrong gradient
    Whether upstream land use has changed
    Whether the lining specification was met
    Whether the drain is still level — toe heave may have taken it out of grade

    Ground moving beneath the road can put a drain out of grade, after which it silts indefinitely however often it is cleaned. It is a displacement symptom, not a maintenance failure.

    13 · Two identical cuts in the same rock, opposite sides of one valley. One sheds blocks every year; the other has stood for twenty. The likeliest reason:

    Joint or bedding orientation relative to the face
    Different intact rock strength
    Different rainfall on the two aspects
    Poorer workmanship on one side

    On one side the discontinuities dip into the hill and cannot release a block; on the other they daylight in the face. Same rock, opposite kinematics — which is why geological reconnaissance belongs before the alignment is fixed.

    14 · Which failure mode is most often misread as favourable?

    Planar sliding
    Wedge sliding
    Toppling
    Circular failure in soil

    Toppling occurs where joints dip into the face — the classic signature of favourable geometry. Where they are steep and closely spaced they form slender columns that rotate forward, and it often precedes a fall.

    15 · Failures along a hill road cluster at drain outlets, hairpins and fill sections. This tells you:

    The geology changes at those points
    The problem is manufactured — those are where the road decides where water goes
    Construction quality was poorer at those locations
    Rainfall is locally intensified by topography there

    A natural hazard has no reason to prefer your outlets. The clustering is the fingerprint of a road-created problem, and drawn on one sketch it is the most persuasive document you can produce about a road.

    16 · A regional susceptibility map at 1:50,000 shows your site as "high". You should:

    Refuse the site — the map is authoritative at that scale
    Ignore it, since regional maps have no site validity
    Average it with your own judgement
    Use it to prioritise a site inspection, which is the only thing that can settle it

    Its cells are tens of metres across and it carries no site-resolution information. It screens where to walk; it cannot clear or condemn a site. Using it as a permit boundary is the commonest misuse in circulation.

    Act III — Water

    17 · The critical storm duration for sizing a culvert equals:

    24 hours, as used in most standards
    The catchment's time of concentration
    The longest recorded storm
    One hour, by convention for small catchments

    Shorter than t𝒸 and the whole catchment never contributes at once; much longer and average intensity has fallen. Option A is the commonest error in hill cross-drainage and it under-designs badly.

    18 · Two catchments, same area, same rainfall. One steep and compact, one long and gentle. Which needs the larger culvert?

    The steep compact one
    The long gentle one
    Both the same — the volume is identical
    Depends only on land cover

    Volume is the same, but a culvert is sized on peak discharge. A short time of concentration with simultaneous arrival produces a much higher, briefer peak. Option C is the trap.

    19 · A structure designed for the 50-year flood, with a 50-year life, will see its design event exceeded with probability:

    2 %
    about 25 %
    about 64 %
    about 98 %

    1 − (1 − 1/50)⁵⁰ ≈ 0.64. More likely than not — which is why the design must also answer what happens when it is exceeded, rather than treating exceedance as a remote contingency.

    20 · You estimate design discharge by two methods and they differ by a factor of three. You should:

    Take the average
    Take the larger, to be safe
    Take the one from the more sophisticated method
    Stop and find the error before choosing

    A discrepancy that large is usually a mistake — wrong catchment area, a units slip, or an intensity at the wrong duration. Averaging two estimates of unknown quality just produces a third of unknown quality.

    21 · The largest single threat to a waterway's adequacy over its life is:

    Error in the return-period selection
    Blockage by debris
    Error in the runoff coefficient
    Uncertainty in the rainfall record

    Blockage can remove most of the opening; bed aggradation comes next. Refining coefficients moves the answer a few per cent — which is why effort belongs in opening size, freeboard and maintainability.

    22 · The Koshi left its course in 2008 at a discharge well below barrage capacity because:

    The barrage gates failed
    An upstream landslide dam released
    Aggradation had perched the bed, and the embankment breached
    Rainfall exceeded the design storm

    Avulsion is a breach mechanism, not a capacity mechanism. A confined aggrading river is being loaded steadily towards the day it takes a lower path — which is why you site for the belt, not the channel.

    23 · Which sediment fraction most concerns a bridge foundation?

    Wash load
    Suspended load
    Dissolved load
    Bed load

    Bed load builds bars, fills the waterway and governs whether the bed aggrades or degrades. Suspended and wash load mostly pass through — they matter to a reservoir, not to your founding level.

    24 · You inspect a pier at low water and the bed looks normal. This tells you:

    Very little — scour peaks at the flood and refills as it recedes
    The foundation is adequate
    Scour has not occurred this season
    The apron is functioning

    The hole fills with loose, uncompacted sediment that looks exactly like bed and has almost no bearing capacity. Probing beside the pier, and repeat bed surveys at the same sections, are what see through it.

    25 · A client asks you to delete one span to save money. Your reply must include:

    That the deck load will increase
    That narrowing the waterway raises contraction scour, so foundations go deeper
    That the bearings will need replacing sooner
    That approach gradients will steepen

    Scour depth rises with discharge intensity to the two-thirds power. Some of the saving simply moves underground — in the worked example, cutting the waterway 25 % drove the founding level 3.5 m deeper.

    26 · A launching apron that has slid down into a scour hole has:

    Failed and must be replaced with a rigid structure
    Been undersized
    Done exactly what it was designed to do
    Indicated foundation settlement

    Launching is its function — it falls into the developing hole and armours it. The real questions are whether the stone was sized for scour-hole velocity and whether anybody has replenished it since.

    27 · Bank protection most often fails by:

    Toe undermining and outflanking at the ends
    Overtopping of the wall
    Wire corrosion in the gabion baskets
    Overturning under earth pressure

    The wall is usually intact when it fails — the bed beneath it went, or the river went round the end. Inspection should therefore start at the toe and the ends, not at the face.

    28 · Which argument for non-stationarity requires no climate projection at all?

    Extreme rainfall is intensifying globally
    Glacial lakes are growing
    Return periods are inherently uncertain
    The catchment has been roaded and cleared since the record was made

    The gauge record was generated by a catchment that no longer exists. It is observable from twenty-year-old imagery, local, and very hard for anyone to dispute.

    Act IV — The Road That Survives

    29 · Alignment is the largest risk-reduction lever on a road because it:

    Determines construction cost more than any other factor
    Is the only decision that changes exposure, and it also governs manufactured hazard
    Fixes the geometric standard
    Sets the maintenance regime

    It moves two of the four risk terms, one of them uniquely — and it is free at corridor stage and impossible after construction, which is the asymmetry that makes it the strongest argument in any project meeting.

    30 · The most valuable and most often omitted drainage element on a hill road is:

    The lined side drain
    Subsurface drainage behind walls
    The catch drain above the cut
    The turnout

    It intercepts hillside runoff before it reaches the cut face at all, preventing both erosion of the face and infiltration into ground the cut has already destabilised. It is cheap and it is usually simply not there.

    31 · Three 900 mm pipes versus one 2.7 m box of similar capacity. Prefer:

    The box — far harder to block
    The pipes — redundancy if one blocks
    The pipes — cheaper and quicker to install
    Either; hydraulically they are equivalent

    Option B is the tempting one and it is backwards: with three pipes a single blockage removes a third of capacity immediately, and round openings are the worst shape for debris.

    32 · A gabion wall is permeable, so drainage behind it:

    Is unnecessary
    Is only needed above 3 m height
    Is provided automatically by the mesh
    Still needs free-draining backfill and a filter, or the wall becomes a dam

    A permeable face is useless if water behind cannot reach it. Backfilled with silty spoil and no filter, fines blind the drainage and full hydrostatic pressure develops — on a structure never designed for it.

    33 · Bio-engineering is the appropriate remedy when:

    The slope is steep and bare, whatever the mechanism
    The failure surface lies within the root zone, roughly the top 0.5–1.5 m
    Structural options are unaffordable
    The site is remote and inaccessible to plant

    Below the root zone nothing reaches the mechanism. Options C and D are how it gets misapplied — and a bio-engineered face that fails deep-seated is then recorded as evidence the technique does not work.

    34 · If you can get only one Green Road principle into a specification, take:

    Labour-based construction
    Staged construction across seasons
    Mass balance and no side-tipping of spoil
    Bio-engineering from the outset

    It removes the largest damage mechanism — spoil loading the slope below, burying natural drainage and stockpiling debris-flow material — and unlike the others it survives being handed to a contractor with an excavator.

    35 · Which bridge element is most often lost in Nepal?

    The approach embankment
    The deck
    The girders
    The bearings

    Outflanking, overtopping and settlement take approaches far more often than anything above the bearing line fails. The superstructure is usually intact when a bridge is lost — which is where design attention has been concentrated.

    36 · A resilient design in a place with no funded maintenance is:

    Still the right choice, since it starts stronger
    Acceptable if the community can be trained
    Preferable because it delays the first failure
    Not resilient — choose the design that survives neglect

    A design whose performance depends on annual work that nobody is funded to do fails on roughly the same schedule as the cheap option, while costing more. Design for the maintenance regime you will actually get.

    Glossary

    Terms whose exact meaning changes the engineering, and the abbreviations you will meet in Nepali practice.

    Terms

    TermMeaning
    AffluxRise in upstream water level caused by a constriction. Freeboard is measured from this level, not from normal flood level
    AggradationBed level rising because sediment supply exceeds transport capacity. Reduces waterway and perches the channel
    AvulsionA river abandoning its channel for a lower path across its floodplain. A breach mechanism, not a capacity failure
    Bed loadCoarse sediment rolling and hopping along the bed. The fraction that fills waterways and governs aggradation
    BraidedMultiple shifting channels around bars. High sediment, erodible banks — there is no single channel to site on
    CapacityAbility to anticipate, absorb and recover. Warning, funds, skills, redundancy, organisation
    ColluviumSlope deposit of previously moved material. Often an old landslide, dormant rather than stable
    Contraction scourBed lowering caused by your structure narrowing the waterway. The component you control directly
    Debris flowA flow in which sediment is the bulk of the material and water makes it mobile. ~1.8–2.3 t/m³
    DegradationBed level falling. Undermines foundations, aprons and protection works
    Diaphragm actionA floor stiff enough in its own plane to distribute seismic force among the vertical elements
    Discharge intensity (q)Discharge per metre of effective waterway. Drives regime scour depth
    DormantA landslide that has moved before under conditions that still exist. The most dangerous activity state, because it looks like stable ground
    DuctilityCapacity to deform substantially without losing strength. What buys time to get out
    Effective stressσ − u. Friction is mobilised by this, not by total stress
    EntrainmentA flow scouring bed and banks as it travels, so it grows on the way down
    ExposureWhat is in the hazard's way. The only risk term reducible to zero
    Factor of safetyResisting force ÷ driving force. 1.0 means it is moving
    General scourLong-term and flood-period lowering of the whole reach, independent of your structure
    GLOFGlacial lake outburst flood. A flood with no weather attached, from a source population that is growing
    Grip lengthFoundation embedment below maximum scour level. For wells, conventionally at least ⅓ of max scour below HFL
    HazardThe damaging phenomenon — its magnitude and frequency. Generally beyond an engineer's influence, with the road-construction exception
    Kinematic testWhether joint geometry physically permits a block to move. If not, the mode is impossible whatever the strength
    Launching apronSacrificial stone laid flat beyond the toe, designed to slide into the scour hole and armour it
    LDOFLandslide dam outburst flood. Landslide dams often fail within days or weeks of forming
    Local scourThe hole excavated around a pier or abutment by the horseshoe vortex. Scales with pier width0.65
    Mass balanceMatching cut to fill along an alignment so there is no spoil to dispose of. The core Green Road principle
    Matric suctionApparent cohesion from surface tension in partly saturated pores. Holds cut faces through the dry season and vanishes on saturation
    Non-stationarityThe flood distribution itself changing, so a historic record no longer describes the future
    OutflankingA river going round the end of protective works. The second great killer after toe scour
    PipingWater tracking along the outside of a culvert barrel or through a fill, carrying fines. Shows as damp patches and settlement
    PoundingAdjacent buildings striking each other in an earthquake, worst where floors are at different levels
    RegimeThe width, depth and slope an alluvial river settles into for a given discharge and bed material
    Short columnA column shortened by partial infill or sloping ground. Stiffer, attracts more force, fails in brittle shear
    Silt factor (f)1.76 √d₅₀ with d₅₀ in millimetres. Coarser bed, higher f, less scour
    Soft storeyAn open ground floor beneath infilled floors, concentrating all seismic drift in one level. The most lethal configuration defect in Nepal
    SusceptibilityWhere a hazard can occur — terrain only. No timing, no consequence, and not a risk map
    Time of concentrationTime for runoff from the most remote point to reach the outlet. Sets the critical storm duration
    Through-stoneA stone spanning both wythes of a masonry wall, preventing it splitting and shedding its outer leaf
    UnseatingA span displacing off its bearing. The dominant seismic collapse mechanism for simply supported bridges
    VulnerabilityHow badly the exposed thing is damaged when the hazard reaches it. Design, detailing, construction, maintenance — and social circumstance

    Sources and honesty

    This handbook was compiled from the public record — Nepali Acts, standards and agency publications; the international earthquake, geotechnical and river engineering literature; and the documented history of the events it cites. It is written for study, not as a design authority.

    Two rules were held throughout. First, values that are code or standard requirements are named as such and attributed to the document, never to an invented clause number — a wrong clause reference is worse than none, because it would be relied on. Where you see a number marked standard, read it from the current edition before using it. Second, everything added on top of the sources — the judgment boxes, the field assignments, the checklists, the worked examples and the four-column costing method — is a study aid built for this book, and is presented as such rather than as established practice.

    The worked examples use illustrative inputs to demonstrate method. Replace every number with your own site's data before using them for anything real.

    Educational use. Not a design authority and not a substitute for the standards themselves.
