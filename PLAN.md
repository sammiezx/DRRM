# The Learning Design

> **Phase 1 document.** How the handbook teaches, in what order, and what keeps him reading.
> [`SCOPE.md`](SCOPE.md) settles *who it is for* and *what it covers*. This settles *how*.
>
> Phase 1 — plan (this). Phase 2 — write. Phase 3 — deploy.

---

## The goal, stated plainly

Make him genuinely **world-class** in disaster risk reduction — and make him *want to
open it tomorrow*.

Those two goals pull against each other, and most study material fails by picking one.
Comprehensive material goes unread. Engaging material stays shallow. The whole design
below exists to hold both at once.

---

## What "world-class" means here

Not "covers more". A summary of a syllabus is worth nothing to a man who has been
building for thirty years. Four things separate world-class from competent, and every
one of them is a writing rule:

**1. The *why* under the code, not the code.**
Anyone can look up a clause. Very few engineers can tell you *why* the clause says what
it says — why the confining stirrups tighten near the joint, why the code punishes an
open ground floor so hard. He should finish able to derive the rule from the physics,
which means he can also tell when the rule doesn't fit the situation in front of him.

**2. Forensics on real buildings.**
Every major idea is taught through something that actually fell down, in a place he can
picture. Not photographs of Turkey — Sitapaila, Gongabu, Barpak, Sankhu, Jajarkot. The
mechanism is the lesson; the building is what makes it stick.

**3. Judgment where textbooks go quiet.**
The interesting questions have no clean answer. Retrofit or rebuild? What do you tell a
family who cannot afford the right thing? Do you sign off on a building that meets the
code but that you don't trust? A world-class engineer has *thought about these before*
the day he must answer one. Each of these gets a box, and none of them gets a tidy answer.

**4. Enough standing to argue.**
He should be able to hold his ground with a municipality engineer, a contractor cutting
corners, or a client who wants the ground floor open for shops — with the clause number,
the mechanism and the failure case all ready. That is what turns knowledge into authority.

## What keeps him reading

Six decisions, in order of how much they matter:

**Story before syllabus.** The handbook is one continuous argument, not eleven topics.
It runs: *the ground is going to move → here is how to see which buildings will die →
here is how to make them stand → here is how to fix the ones already up → here is
everything past the building.* Each session ends somewhere that makes the next one the
obvious question.

**Sessions, not chapters.** A "chapter" is open-ended and easy to postpone. A **session**
is a finishable thing: 12–20 minutes, one idea, done. He can complete one waiting for
tea. Every session carries its honest reading time at the top.

**One idea per session, stated out loud.** Each session names its single idea in one
sentence, near the top. If he reads nothing else, he takes that away.

**Something to do.** Every session ends with one small **field assignment** — usually
fifteen minutes, in the real world, not on paper. This is the piece that converts reading
into seeing. After session 9 he will not be able to walk past an open ground floor again
without noticing it. That change is the whole point.

**Something to check.** Then three to five **Check yourself** questions with answers
hidden until tapped. Retrieval, not recognition — he has to produce the answer before
seeing it. This is the single best-evidenced study technique there is, and it costs him
two minutes.

**Visible progress, quietly.** The site remembers where he is and offers **Continue** on
the cover. A progress page shows the 32 sessions with what's done. No streaks, no badges,
no confetti — he is a professional, not a child. Just an honest record of ground covered.

## The shape of a session

Every session has the same six-part rhythm. Sameness is the point: he learns the rhythm
once and afterwards always knows where he is and how much is left.

| | Part | Length | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The hook** | 100–150 words | A real building, a real day. Ends on a question |
| 2 | **The idea** | one sentence | The single thing this session teaches, in a box |
| 3 | **The body** | 800–1400 words | Working depth. Figures where a figure beats a paragraph. Clause numbers where a number will be relied on |
| 4 | **Judgment** | 100–200 words | What he does differently on Monday. Where the honest answer is "it depends", it says so |
| 5 | **Field assignment** | ~15 min | One thing to do outside. See below |
| 6 | **Check yourself** | 3–5 Q | Tap to reveal. Retrieval practice |

**Prose rules.** Short paragraphs, one idea each. Concrete before abstract — the building
first, the principle second. No word he wouldn't use himself. Nothing padded to look
thorough. If a sentence isn't doing work, it goes.

## The assignments

The engagement mechanism that matters most, and the easiest to get wrong. Rules:

- **Fifteen minutes, and outside.** Not a problem set. Something in the world.
- **Uses the street he already walks.** No lab, no software, no travel.
- **Produces something** — a sketch, a count, a photo, a decision written down.
- **No right answer to fail against.** Every assignment gets a *"what you probably found"*
  note, so he can compare rather than be graded.

Six kinds, rotated so it never gets stale:

| Kind | Example |
|---|---|
| **Look** | Walk your street. Find three buildings with an open ground floor. Which is worst, and why? |
| **Sketch** | Draw the load path of your own house, floor by floor, down to the soil |
| **Count** | Count the storeys added to any building since it was built. Ask the owner if the columns were designed for them |
| **Measure** | Pace out a building's plan. Is the stiffness centred, or is the staircase pulling it to one side? |
| **Ask** | Ask a mason how he decides where to stop a wall. Compare with NBC 202 |
| **Decide** | You are asked to certify a 1990s school. You have half a day and no drawings. Write your method in ten lines |

By the end he has walked his own town as a risk assessor. That is worth more than any
chapter.

## The 32 sessions

Five acts. Roughly 12–20 minutes each — **two a week is four months; three a week is
under three.** Sessions marked **◆** are the heavy engineering core.

### Act I — The Ground *(5 sessions)*
*Why this matters, before any engineering.*

| # | Session | The idea it lands |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The eighty-year clock** | 1934, 2015, and the segment still locked. It is not *if* |
| 2 | **Risk is built, not suffered** | Hazard × exposure × vulnerability ÷ capacity. The only term an engineer can move is vulnerability — and that is the whole job |
| 3 | **The other hazards** | Flood, GLOF, landslide, fire — and the cascades, where one hazard makes the next |
| 4 | **What 2015 actually taught** | The damage patterns. What stood, what fell, and the uncomfortable reasons |
| 5 | **The rules of the game** | DRRM Act 2074, NDRRMA, three tiers, and where NBC gets its legal teeth |

### Act II — Reading Buildings *(7 sessions)*
*Learning to see a building's death before it happens.*

| # | Session | The idea it lands |
|---|---|---|
| 6 | **The five buildings of Nepal** | Stone-in-mud, brick-in-mud, brick-in-cement, RC-with-infill, and the hybrid nobody designed. Each dies its own way |
| 7 ◆ | **Inertia** | The earthquake does not push the building. The building's own mass does |
| 8 ◆ | **Following the force down** | Load path, diaphragm, redundancy. Break the chain anywhere and the whole thing is decoration |
| 9 ◆ | **Soft storey and short column** | The two killers you can see from the street |
| 10 ◆ | **Torsion, irregularity, pounding** | When a plan on paper becomes a mechanism in the ground |
| 11 ◆ | **The ground beneath** | Liquefaction in the valley, amplification, slope failure. The best frame on the wrong soil |
| 12 | **Twenty minutes, one score** | Rapid Visual Screening — turning an engineer's eye into a number someone else can act on |

### Act III — Making It Stand *(9 sessions)*
*The craft. The longest act, and the one he will re-read.*

| # | Session | The idea it lands |
|---|---|---|
| 13 ◆ | **Strength, stiffness, ductility** | Three different things. Only one of them saves lives |
| 14 ◆ | **Deciding where it breaks** | Capacity design. Strong column, weak beam — choosing the failure you can survive |
| 15 ◆ | **NBC 105:2020** | What changed from 1994, and what the change is protecting against |
| 16 ◆ | **From map to force** | Hazard map → spectrum → design coefficient. Every step, with the reason |
| 17 ◆ | **Worked example** | Base shear for a four-storey frame in Kathmandu. Every number sourced |
| 18 ◆ | **Detailing that decides it** | IS 13920 and the handful of site rules that separate ductile from brittle |
| 19 ◆ | **Masonry that survives** | Bands, corners, through-stones — and *why* each one works |
| 20 ◆ | **Building without analysis** | MRT, NBC 201 and 202. Rules of thumb that are not thumbs — where they come from and where they stop |
| 21 | **Past the code** | Base isolation, damping. What they cost and when they are honestly worth it in Nepal |

### Act IV — Strengthening What Stands *(6 sessions)*
*Most of the buildings that will be here in twenty years are already here.*

| # | Session | The idea it lands |
|---|---|---|
| 22 | **Repair, restore, strengthen, replace** | Four different decisions. Most engineers conflate them |
| 23 ◆ | **Finding out what you have** | Survey, NDT, material testing. Working without drawings, which is most of the time |
| 24 ◆ | **Retrofitting concrete** | Jacketing, added shear walls, bracing, FRP. What each one actually changes |
| 25 ◆ | **Retrofitting masonry** | Splint-and-bandage, ferrocement, stitching, grouting, through-stone insertion |
| 26 | **Schools, hospitals, heritage** | Nepal's three special cases, and why each needs a different argument |
| 27 | **What it costs** | Cost–benefit, and the honest point where retrofit stops making sense |

### Act V — Beyond the Building *(5 sessions)*
*Where an engineer becomes useful to a whole community.*

| # | Session | The idea it lands |
|---|---|---|
| 28 | **The things that fall on people** | Non-structural risk. The structure holds and the building still kills |
| 29 | **Lifelines** | Roads, bridges, water, power. A safe house on a cut-off hillside |
| 30 | **The first 72 hours** | The engineer's actual job after the shaking. Rapid assessment and the green/yellow/red card |
| 31 | **Building back better** | The NRA experience, told honestly — owner-driven reconstruction, what worked, what did not |
| 32 | **Land, money, the next one** | Risk-sensitive land use, financing, insurance. Closing the loop back to session 2 |

**Plus, not sessions:**

- **Fast Track** — the whole argument in twenty minutes. Written *last*, because it is a
  compression of the finished book. For the day he wants to remind himself, or explain it
  to someone else.
- **Practice Kit** — field checklists, must-know numbers and clauses, the full question
  bank, glossary, and where to download every code cited. Reference, not reading.

## Practice, in three layers

Three different jobs, three different tools:

| Layer | Where | Job |
|---|---|---|
| **Check yourself** | End of every session | Immediate retrieval, while it's fresh. 3–5 questions, tap to reveal |
| **Flashcards** | Anytime, from the toolbar | Spaced recall of things that must be automatic — clause numbers, coefficients, spacings, failure modes. Six decks |
| **Question bank** | Practice Kit | Longer problems and judgment scenarios. ~40 MCQs with explanations, plus open questions with model answers |

The MCQs exist to *teach*, not to score. Every wrong option is a real misconception, and
every explanation says why the wrong answer is tempting.

## What the site does to help

Built in Phase 2, on the chassis that is already in place:

- **Continue** on the cover — goes straight to the next unfinished session
- **Progress page** — all 32 sessions, what's read, what assignments are done
- **Reading time** on every session, honestly estimated
- **Tap to reveal** answers, so a page can be read without spoiling itself
- **Assignment log** — tick it done, and jot what he found; saved on his phone, nowhere else
- **Session stepper** — previous / next at the foot of every session, so the drawer is rarely needed
- Everything already there: full-text search, flashcards, dark mode, offline, print to PDF

All state is `localStorage` on his own phone. No account, no server, nothing leaves the
device.

## Phase 2 — the writing order

Sessions are written in dependency order, not numeric order:

1. **Act II (6–12)** — the diagnostic eye. Everything later assumes it, and it is the most
   satisfying act to read first if he dips in early.
2. **Act III (13–21)** — the craft. Heaviest; write it while fresh. Session 17's worked
   example gets its numbers checked twice.
3. **Act IV (22–27)** — strengthening. Leans on II and III throughout.
4. **Act I (1–5)** — the ground. Written after the engineering, so the hooks can point
   forward to real sessions rather than promises.
5. **Act V (28–32)** — beyond the building.
6. **Fast Track** — a compression of the finished thing. Cannot be written earlier.
7. **Practice Kit** — harvested from all 32: every number, clause, checklist and
   abbreviation already written, collected.

Figures ([13 planned](content/PHASE2.md)) are drawn alongside the session that needs them.
