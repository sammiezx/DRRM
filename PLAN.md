# The Learning Design

> **Phase 1 document.** How the handbook teaches, in what order, and what keeps him reading.
> [`SCOPE.md`](SCOPE.md) settles *who it is for* and *what it covers*. This settles *how*.
>
> Phase 1 — plan (this). Phase 2 — write. Phase 3 — deploy.

---

## The goal, stated plainly

Make him genuinely **world-class** in disaster risk reduction — and make him *want to
open it tomorrow*.

Those two goals pull against each other, and most study material fails by picking one.
Comprehensive material goes unread. Engaging material stays shallow. The whole design
below exists to hold both at once.

---

## What "world-class" means here

Not "covers more". A summary of a syllabus is worth nothing to a man who has been
building roads for thirty years. Four things separate world-class from competent, and every
one of them is a writing rule:

**1. The *why* under the standard, not the standard.**
Anyone can look up a clause. Very few engineers can tell you *why* the clause says what
it says — why the drain has to be lined *before* the slope is cut, why a
culvert sized on discharge alone still blocks, why a gabion wall fails from behind. He
should finish able to derive the rule from the mechanics, which means he can also tell
when the rule does not fit the ground in front of him.

**2. Forensics on real failures.**
Every major idea is taught through something that actually failed, in a place he can
picture. Not case studies from Switzerland — Sunkoshi 2014, Melamchi 2021, Thame 2024,
the Prithvi and Narayanghat–Mugling slides, the bridges lost to scour last monsoon. The
mechanism is the lesson; the place is what makes it stick.

**3. Judgment where textbooks go quiet.**
The interesting questions have no clean answer. Do you realign at ten times the cost, or
armour a line you know is wrong? What do you tell a village whose only road is the thing
destabilising their hillside? Do you certify a bridge that meets the standard but sits in
a channel you have watched migrate? A world-class engineer has *thought about these before*
the day he must answer one. Each of these gets a box, and none of them gets a tidy answer.

**4. Enough standing to argue.**
He should be able to hold his ground with a contractor tipping spoil down a slope, a
ward chair who wants the alignment moved for a shortcut, or a donor asking why resilient
costs more per kilometre — with the standard, the mechanism and the failure case all
ready. That is what turns knowledge into authority.

## What keeps him reading

Six decisions, in order of how much they matter:

**Story before syllabus.** The handbook is one continuous argument, not a list of topics.
It runs: *this ground is unstable and getting more so → here is how to read a slope before
it fails → here is how water actually behaves in these catchments → here is how to put a
road through it that survives → here is everything past the alignment.* Each session ends
somewhere that makes the next one the obvious question.

**Sessions, not chapters.** A "chapter" is open-ended and easy to postpone. A **session**
is a finishable thing: 12–20 minutes, one idea, done. He can complete one waiting for
tea. Every session carries its honest reading time at the top.

**One idea per session, stated out loud.** Each session names its single idea in one
sentence, near the top. If he reads nothing else, he takes that away.

**Something to do.** Every session ends with one small **field assignment** — usually
fifteen minutes, in the real world, not on paper. This is the piece that converts reading
into seeing. After session 10 he should not be able to drive a hill road again without
reading where its water goes. That change is the whole point.

**Something to check.** Then three to five **Check yourself** questions with answers
hidden until tapped. Retrieval, not recognition — he has to produce the answer before
seeing it. This is the single best-evidenced study technique there is, and it costs him
two minutes.

**Visible progress, quietly.** The site remembers where he is and offers **Continue** on
the cover. A progress page shows the 32 sessions with what's done. No streaks, no badges,
no confetti — he is a professional, not a child. Just an honest record of ground covered.

## The shape of a session

Every session has the same six-part rhythm. Sameness is the point: he learns the rhythm
once and afterwards always knows where he is and how much is left.

| | Part | Length | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The hook** | 100–150 words | A real place, a real day, something that failed. Ends on a question |
| 2 | **The idea** | one sentence | The single thing this session teaches, in a box |
| 3 | **The body** | 800–1400 words | Working depth. Figures where a figure beats a paragraph. Clause numbers where a number will be relied on |
| 4 | **Judgment** | 100–200 words | What he does differently on Monday. Where the honest answer is "it depends", it says so |
| 5 | **Field assignment** | ~15 min | One thing to do outside. See below |
| 6 | **Check yourself** | 3–5 Q | Tap to reveal. Retrieval practice |

**Prose rules.** Short paragraphs, one idea each. Concrete before abstract — the slope
first, the principle second. No word he wouldn't use himself. Nothing padded to look
thorough. If a sentence isn't doing work, it goes.

## The assignments

The engagement mechanism that matters most, and the easiest to get wrong. Rules:

- **Fifteen minutes, and outside.** Not a problem set. Something in the world.
- **Uses the roads he already drives.** No lab, no software, no special trip.
- **Produces something** — a sketch, a count, a photo, a decision written down.
- **No right answer to fail against.** Every assignment gets a *"what you probably found"*
  note, so he can compare rather than be graded.

Six kinds, rotated so it never gets stale:

| Kind | Example |
|---|---|
| **Look** | Walk 500 m of any hill road. Find every place the drainage discharges straight onto a slope. Photograph the worst |
| **Sketch** | Draw a section through a cut you pass often — slope angle, bench, drain, spoil. Mark where the water goes |
| **Count** | Count culverts along one kilometre. How many are blocked? What does that spacing say about the design discharge |
| **Measure** | Pace the span and look at a bridge pier at low water. Is there a scour hole? How deep, and is the apron still there |
| **Ask** | Ask an overseer where this road always fails in monsoon. He will know to the metre. Then work out why |
| **Decide** | A slide has taken 40 m of road. You have one excavator and three weeks to monsoon. Write your method in ten lines |

By the end he will have re-read his own district as a risk assessor. That is worth more
than any chapter.

## The 32 sessions

Five acts. Roughly 12–20 minutes each — **two a week is four months; three a week is
under three.** Sessions marked **◆** are the technical core.

The weighting follows his career: **22 of 32 sessions are slopes, water and roads.**
Buildings get one compressed session — enough to judge a school and hold an argument —
because that is not where his work is.

### Act I — The Ground *(5 sessions)*
*Why this matters, before any engineering.*

| # | Session | The idea it lands |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The eighty-year clock** | 1934, 2015, and the segment still locked. For a road engineer the earthquake arrives as ten thousand landslides |
| 2 | **Risk is built, not suffered** | Hazard × exposure × vulnerability ÷ capacity. The only term an engineer can move is vulnerability — that is the whole job |
| 3 | **Why Nepal's hills move** | Young rock, steep ground, four metres of monsoon, a range still rising. The four belts behave differently and want different engineering |
| 4 | **The rules of the game — Act, agency, standard** | DRRM Act 2074, NDRRMA, three tiers — and where DoR and DoLI standards get their legal teeth |
| 5 | **The cascade — one hazard triggers the next** | Earthquake → landslide → river blockage → outburst flood. Sunkoshi 2014, Melamchi 2021. Hazards arrive as chains |

### Act II — Reading the Terrain *(7 sessions)*
*Learning to see a slope fail before it does.*

| # | Session | The idea it lands |
|---|---|---|
| 6 ◆ | **How a slope fails** | Shear strength, pore pressure, factor of safety. Rain is not the cause — it is the trigger |
| 7 ◆ | **The five ways ground moves** | Fall, topple, slide, spread, flow. What each looks like on a hillside, and what each demands |
| 8 ◆ | **Debris flow** | A different animal from a landslide, and the one that takes bridges out |
| 9 ◆ | **Reading a hillside** | Tension cracks, seepage lines, tilted trees, hummocky ground, a spring that moved |
| 10 ◆ | **The road as the cause** | Cut slopes, spoil tipping, concentrated drainage. How a badly built road manufactures landslides for fifty years |
| 11 ◆ | **Rock, not soil** | Rock mass behaviour, planar and wedge failure, rockfall and what actually stops it |
| 12 | **Susceptibility on a map** | Turning terrain judgment into something a planner can act on |

### Act III — Water *(7 sessions)*
*Hydrology as a hazard discipline, not a chapter in a textbook.*

| # | Session | The idea it lands |
|---|---|---|
| 13 ◆ | **The catchment** | Time of concentration, and why the small catchments are the ones that kill roads |
| 14 ◆ | **Design discharge — the number you are judged on** | Rainfall-runoff, return periods, design discharge. Choosing it, and defending it |
| 15 ◆ | **How rivers behave** | Sediment, braiding, aggradation, avulsion, bank erosion. Nepal's rivers are not textbook rivers |
| 16 ◆ | **Flood, flash flood, GLOF** | Three different problems with three different warning times. Thame 2024, Melamchi 2021 |
| 17 ◆ | **Scour** | The single largest killer of bridges in Nepal. General, contraction, local — estimating it, and what to do |
| 18 ◆ | **Training the river** | Spurs, revetments, gabions, guide bunds. What holds and what washes out in the first monsoon |
| 19 | **A design flood that keeps moving** | Non-stationarity — the honest problem with sizing tomorrow's structure from yesterday's record |

### Act IV — The Road That Survives *(8 sessions)*
*The craft. The longest act, and the one he will re-read.*

| # | Session | The idea it lands |
|---|---|---|
| 20 ◆ | **Alignment is the decision** | Where the line goes decides what you fight for fifty years. The biggest DRR choice on any road |
| 21 ◆ | **Drainage is everything** | Side drains, turnouts, spacing. The largest single cause of rural road failure in Nepal, and the cheapest to fix |
| 22 ◆ | **Cross-drainage** | Culverts, causeways, vented fords. Sizing, the afflux trap, and blockage as the real design case |
| 23 ◆ | **Walls that hold** | Gabion, dry stone, RCC breast and retaining walls. Choosing between them — and the drainage behind them that actually decides it |
| 24 ◆ | **Bio-engineering** | Nepal's own contribution to the field. What roots do mechanically, what they cannot do, how to combine them with structure |
| 25 ◆ | **Green Roads** | Labour-based, environment-friendly local roads. The standard, and the logic that produced it |
| 26 ◆ | **Bridges under hazard** | Approach and abutment failure, liquefaction, bearings and expansion. Where seismic design actually bites on a bridge |
| 27 | **Resilience, honestly costed** | Build once against maintain forever. What resilience is worth per kilometre, and when it is not |

### Act V — Beyond the Alignment *(5 sessions)*
*Where an engineer becomes useful to a whole district.*

| # | Session | The idea it lands |
|---|---|---|
| 28 | **Roads are the lifeline** | Access *is* the response. Critical links, redundancy, what a district looks like when one bridge goes |
| 29 | **The first 72 hours** | Clearance, route triage, temporary crossings, Bailey bridges. The road engineer's actual job |
| 30 ◆ | **Buildings, compressed** | Load path, soft storey, NBC 105, retrofit basics. Enough to judge a school and hold the argument — the whole building world in one session |
| 31 | **Building back better** | Reconstruction told honestly — the NRA experience, and what resilient road recovery looks like |
| 32 | **Land use, money, and the next one** | Risk-sensitive land use, maintenance funding, insurance. Closing the loop back to session 2 |

**Plus, not sessions:**

- **Fast Track** — the whole argument in twenty minutes. Written *last*, because it is a
  compression of the finished book. For the day he wants to remind himself, or explain it
  to someone else.
- **Practice Kit** — field checklists, must-know numbers and clauses, the full question
  bank, glossary, and where to download every standard cited. Reference, not reading.

## Practice, in three layers

Three different jobs, three different tools:

| Layer | Where | Job |
|---|---|---|
| **Check yourself** | End of every session | Immediate retrieval, while it's fresh. 3–5 questions, tap to reveal |
| **Flashcards** | Anytime, from the toolbar | Spaced recall of things that must be automatic — clause numbers, coefficients, spacings, failure modes. Six decks |
| **Question bank** | Practice Kit | Longer problems and judgment scenarios. ~40 MCQs with explanations, plus open questions with model answers |

The MCQs exist to *teach*, not to score. Every wrong option is a real misconception, and
every explanation says why the wrong answer is tempting.

## What the site does to help

Built in Phase 2, on the chassis that is already in place:

- **Continue** on the cover — goes straight to the next unfinished session
- **Progress page** — all 32 sessions, what's read, what assignments are done
- **Reading time** on every session, honestly estimated
- **Tap to reveal** answers, so a page can be read without spoiling itself
- **Assignment log** — tick it done, and jot what he found; saved on his phone, nowhere else
- **Session stepper** — previous / next at the foot of every session, so the drawer is rarely needed
- Everything already there: full-text search, flashcards, dark mode, offline, print to PDF

All state is `localStorage` on his own phone. No account, no server, nothing leaves the
device.

## Phase 2 — the writing order

Sessions are written in dependency order, not numeric order:

1. **Act II (6–12)** — the terrain eye. Everything later assumes it, and it is the most
   satisfying act to read first if he dips in early.
2. **Act III (13–19)** — water. Second because Act IV's drainage, culvert and scour
   sessions all lean on it.
3. **Act IV (20–27)** — the road. Heaviest act; write it while both II and III are fresh.
4. **Act I (1–5)** — the ground. Written after the engineering, so the hooks can point
   forward to real sessions rather than promises.
5. **Act V (28–32)** — beyond the alignment. Session 30 (buildings, compressed) is written
   last of these, since it has to be ruthless about what to leave out.
6. **Fast Track** — a compression of the finished thing. Cannot be written earlier.
7. **Practice Kit** — harvested from all 32: every number, clause, checklist and
   abbreviation already written, collected.

Figures ([listed in content/PHASE2.md](content/PHASE2.md)) are drawn alongside the session
that needs them.
